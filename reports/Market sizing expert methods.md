# Build market sizings an investor can rebuild

Across venture investors, sell-side critics, consultants and operators, one standard holds: a credible TAM is a derivation a skeptical reader can rebuild from a stated customer count and a stated price, not a number lifted from a report. The primary route is bottom-up (accounts that match the ideal customer profile × what each pays per year, by tier), and top-down (a MECE tree from a population or spend pool, or a third-party market figure) earns its place only as a sanity check on magnitude ([a16z](https://a16z.com/16-more-startup-metrics/); [Pear VC](https://pear.vc/market-sizing-guide/)). Two structurally independent routes that land within tens of percent of each other are the practitioner's definition of "credible"; a larger gap is not a failure but a diagnostic that must be traced to one assumption or a definitional mismatch such as today's served spend versus the total opportunity ([Stanford Biodesign](https://biodesignguide.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Top-Down-and-Bottom-Up-Market-Sizing-Example.pdf)). The named failure modes are consistent across every source: "1% of a huge market", analyst TAMs pasted without derivation, sizing the problem or the adjacent industry instead of the ICP, trillion-dollar TAMs, false precision, and expansion stories without evidence ([TechCrunch, Reichert](https://techcrunch.com/?p=2443773)); the analyst-side adds Damodaran's possible/plausible/probable tiers and the aggregation test (do all players' implied revenues exceed the market?) ([Damodaran](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/blog/PelotonIPO.pdf)). The 2014 Uber debate is the canonical worked example: Damodaran's top-down $100B taxi market produced a $5.9B value, Gurley's bottom-up rebuild from San Francisco evidence and car-ownership substitution produced a $450B–$1.3T TAM, and Uber's actual 2024 gross bookings of $162.8B were nine times Damodaran's projection for Uber and nearly equal to his projection for the entire 2024 taxi market, while profitability and local network effects vindicated his caution and half of today's business is delivery that neither side modelled ([Uber FY2024](https://investor.uber.com/news-events/news/press-release-details/2025/Uber-Announces-Results-for-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2024/default.aspx)). Uncertainty is handled with calibrated 90% intervals per factor (untrained estimators hit only 30–60%), a Monte Carlo rather than multiplied point estimates, a tornado chart to decide what to research next, and an assumptions register with source, date, grain and counter-case ([McKenzie et al. 2008](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~cmckenzie/McKenzieetal2008OBHDP.pdf)). The counting stack is free and official (Census SUSB/CBP/NES, BLS OEWS, Eurostat SBS, ONS, SEC XBRL) but carries 1–4 year lags that the register must record.

## Investors buy the derivation, not the number

The three-letter vocabulary originates with Sequoia's business-plan outline, whose Market Size slide reads in full "Identify/profile the customer you cater to" and "Calculate the TAM (top down), SAM (bottoms up) and SOM" ([Sequoia template, PitchDeckCoach reproduction](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/1126518382322406/Sequoia%20Capital_pitchdecktemplate.pdf)); that single line is the likely source of the "show both" convention. Sequoia's current page has been cut to one sentence, "Identify your customer and your market. Some of the best companies invent their own markets," with no mention of TAM or SAM at all ([Sequoia](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan/)). What replaced the funnel is a provenance test. a16z's 2015 metrics guide states the firm-level rule: "we like seeing a bottoms-up analysis, which takes into account your target customer profile," and it rejects the top-down toothbrush example (1.36B people × $1 × 40% share = $540M) because "This analysis not only tends to overstate market size (why 40%?)" and ignores the difficulty of distribution; the same piece warns founders not to "game" the TAM and reminds them that eBay and Airbnb "sought to address what appeared to be modest TAMs in the beginning" ([a16z](https://a16z.com/16-more-startup-metrics/)).

The consensus formula is simple and the sources state it almost identically. Underscore VC: "Bottom-up market sizing = [# of Customers] * [Price They're Willing to Pay]" ([Underscore VC](https://underscore.vc/resources/bottom-up-market-size-slide/)). Pear VC: "Multiply the number of customers by the average revenue per customer per year," with the crispest rule for the other route, "Ideally you will only use top-down to sanity check the magnitude of your bottom-up estimate," and an instruction to present both the TAM and five-year revenue bottom-up ([Pear VC](https://pear.vc/market-sizing-guide/)). Octopus Ventures defines TAM as "the total number of customers a company can possibly win within its ideal customer profile, multiplied by the price it can charge," recommends a single number, and calls SAM/SOM "somewhat academic" ([Octopus Ventures](https://octopusventures.com/blog/we-need-to-talk-about-tam/)); PSG's Rotem Shacham makes the same point from the growth-equity side, warning that each extra funnel layer is "just adding more and more layers of assumptions and hypotheticals," and gives the fullest one-line version, average price × number of customers × penetration rate ([Fusion VC](https://blog.fusion-vc.com/p/masterclass-market-sizing)). The composite that a method doc can state honestly is therefore **TAM = Σ over tiers of (ICP accounts in tier) × (annual price per account in tier)**, where price may itself be seats × price per seat or usage × unit price; SAM is the tiers and geographies you can serve with today's product and channels; SOM, if shown at all, is a stated and justified penetration applied to SAM.

Two things make this formula load-bearing rather than cosmetic. The first is the unit. Steve Blank, following Ulwick, defines a market as "a group of people and the job they are trying to get done," so that "the market definition becomes a constant in the product/market fit equation, not a variable," and explicitly says not to count job titles, influencers or economic buyers ([Steve Blank, 2021](https://steveblank.com/2021/11/04/market-definition-its-the-front-end-of-customer-discovery/)); Ulwick adds that markets defined this way "are stable over time, as they are solution independent" ([Strategyn](https://strategyn.com/bring-markets-perfect-focus-defining-around-customers-job-done/)). The practical consequence is that the count and the price must share a unit (accounts, seats or job executions), and that the right denominator for "companies that could buy" is the enterprise, not the establishment or the employee. The second is the value metric: OpenView's pricing work lists "You picked the wrong value metric" among the top pricing mistakes and asks "Are seats the right metric for you?", with examples of per-active-user and per-square-foot pricing replacing per-seat or per-building ([Bartlett & Poyar, SaaStr 2018](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/mastering-saas-pricing-saastr-annual-2018/87582858)). Brian Balfour's 2025 update of model-market fit shows why this matters now: "$100M requires about 100,000 customers paying $1,000 per year," but AI products that charge per outcome break the seats arithmetic, so Intercom's Fin at $0.99 per resolution "needs to resolve just over 100m tickets per year" to reach that line ([Reforge](https://www.reforge.com/blog/four-fits-growth-framework)).

The reachability check that investors actually run is not a SOM percentage but a path to $100M of revenue. Christoph Janz's ladder is the canonical version: $100M needs "1,000 enterprise customers paying you $100k+ per year each," 10,000 at $10k+, 100,000 at $1k+, 1 million consumers at $100+, or 10 million ad-monetised users at $10+, and each rung drags a funnel behind it (10,000 mid-market customers need "100,000 or more leads"; 100,000 small businesses need "0.5–2 million trial signups"; 10 million active users need "roughly 100 million people who download your app") ([Christoph Janz](http://christophjanz.blogspot.com/2014/10/five-ways-to-build-100-million-business.html)). Jason Lemkin's version is "Build a REAL, Bottom-Up Model, That Shows How You'll Get to $100m in ARR in 7 Years… And make sure you believe it," with a worked pitch line of "50,000 enterprise customers at an average $50K ACV, representing a $2.5B opportunity," and the summary rule that "The market size number matters far less than how you calculated it" ([SaaStr, 2013](https://www.saastr.com/tam-is-great-but-what-really-matters-is-that-you-believe-you-can-hit-100m-in-7-years/); [SaaStr, YC pitching guide](https://www.saastr.com/the-y-combinator-guide-to-perfectly-pitching-your-seed-stage-startup-with-michael-seibel/)). Alex Iskold's examples show the test in action: 180,000 US dental practices × $6K ARR is ~$1B, so $100M requires retaining 18,000 practices; 5,000 US universities × $100K is $500M and "too small" for most VCs; 120M US households × $240 for Netflix gives $28.8B against roughly $7B of actual US revenue, a reminder that the formula sizes the ceiling, not the outcome ([Alex Iskold](https://www.startuphacks.vc/blog/market-size)). The venture bar itself is stated inconsistently (Pear: "hundreds of millions of dollars of high-margin revenue" within a decade inside a "multiple billion dollar market"; Fusion VC/PSG: roughly $1–2B; Lenny Rachitsky's roundup: "$5B or more"; Iskold: VCs like $10B+ and dislike under $1B), so a method doc should treat the threshold as a parameter the user sets for their investor, not a constant ([Pear VC](https://pear.vc/market-sizing-guide/); [Fusion VC](https://blog.fusion-vc.com/p/masterclass-market-sizing); [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/your-startup-idea-probably-isnt-venture)).

Jared Sleeper of Matrix Partners adds a third route that the PM side needs for new categories: "value theory," which asks "here's how much value we can add, and why we'll be able to capture it," and which he calls "more of a dark art, but nonetheless useful"; his Uber sketch compares the alternatives (staying put, walking, transit, taxis, driving) and asks what a user would pay to be driven instead and "how much of that value pricing can capture" ([Jared Sleeper, forEntrepreneurs](https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/calculating-tam/)). Pear's rule of thumb that "you can charge in the range of 10-30% of the value you create for your customer" is the only named-investor capture ratio found; Ramanujam and Tacke's *Monetizing Innovation* says only that value should be "vastly higher than the price" and gives no percentage, so any capture band in a method doc is a heuristic, not a standard ([Pear VC](https://pear.vc/market-sizing-guide/); [Brikman, notes on Monetizing Innovation](https://www.ybrikman.com/blog/2023/04/01/monetizing-innovation/)).

## Top-down works when it is a MECE tree with justified, rounded leaves

The consulting tradition supplies the discipline that keeps a top-down from being "X% of a huge market." The clearest free statement is StrategyCase's routine by a former McKinsey consultant: clarify the unit, geography and period; structure the problem into "three to five drivers that multiply together" and say the structure aloud before calculating; make "simple, rounded assumptions," each justified "with a benchmark, personal behavior, or basic logic"; calculate without "false precision"; sanity-check by comparing "with a known benchmark, triangulate with a second structure, or check the implied per-unit figure"; and interpret the number for the business including its key sensitivity ([StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/?p=37611)). The same guide's rules are quotable verbatim for an agent: "Round aggressively: 10%, 25%, 50% are far easier to work with than 12.7%"; "Never pull a number from thin air without a one-line justification"; "A number you have not sanity-checked is a liability." Its accuracy yardstick is the only published one from the case tradition: an answer "within the right order of magnitude generally passes, and landing within about 25% of reality is excellent," and its own worked estimates landed within 3–27% of published actuals (German new cars ~2.9M vs 2.82M; Italian weddings ~220k vs 173,272; Heathrow passengers ~80M vs 83.9M) ([StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/?p=37611)). The structural rule underneath is MECE, mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, developed by Barbara Minto at McKinsey in the late 1960s ([Wikipedia: MECE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MECE_principle)).

The guide also catalogues the standard trees, which an agent can offer as a menu once it knows what kind of thing is being sized.

| Situation | Structure | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer market with a clean starting population | Population × % target × usage × price | National food or beverage spend |
| Business market with countable buyers | Units × usage per unit × price | Accounts × seats × price per seat |
| Supply limited by a bottleneck | Capacity per unit × units × utilization | A single restaurant, clinic or warehouse |
| Durables in a mature market | Installed base ÷ years each lasts, plus growth | Replacement demand for equipment |
| One-time life events | Population × share affected ÷ years per lifetime | Weddings, first homes |
| Supply implied by demand | Total demand ÷ output per unit | Number of fuel stations, tuners |

The same product can require different structures at different scales (a national pizza market is top-down from population, a single restaurant from kitchen capacity) ([StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/?p=37611)). The sanity check that pairs with any tree is the per-capita restatement: express 300 piano tuners as "one tuner per 28,000 people" and ask whether that is plausible against "a reference point you already trust" ([Hacking the Case Interview](https://www.hackingthecaseinterview.com/pages/estimation-techniques-case-interview)).

The Fermi literature explains why decomposition works and when it does not. Weinstein and Adam's *Guesstimation* method rounds "to the nearest power of ten," breaks problems into "simpler, more manageable ones," bounds each unknown with an absurdly low and absurdly high pair, and combines bounds with the geometric mean rather than the arithmetic mean ([Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691129495/guesstimation)). The evidence is conditional: MacGregor and Armstrong's 1994 reanalysis and new experiment (10 problems, 280 subjects, 1,078 estimates) found decomposition improved accuracy for targets with "extreme, uncertain" values and "otherwise it often produced less accurate predictions" ([UPenn repository](https://upenn-test.atmire.com/entities/publication/81519b4c-531b-450f-91f5-fc473a09d275)), while Goodwin and Wright warned that decomposition "may actually reduce" accuracy when the sub-judgments are less familiar than the whole ([EA Forum review](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/beRtXkMpCT39y8bPj/there-is-little-evidence-on-question-decomposition)). Market sizes are exactly the extreme, uncertain targets where decomposition pays, but only if each leaf is genuinely easier to estimate than the whole, which is Hubbard's rule for choosing a decomposition in the first place ([LessWrong summary of Hubbard](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ybYBCK9D7MZCcdArB/how-to-measure-anything)). The 2026 collective-estimation experiments add that groups instructed to decompose outperform groups that merely combine guesses, so an agent that forces a user to walk the tree is doing the thing the evidence supports ([UTDT repository](https://repositorio.utdt.edu/items/721cc53d-66a4-45c8-ab56-83e928f20a97/full)).

A disciplined top-down also nests its definitions explicitly. Damodaran's 2015 Uber deck lays out a ladder of market definitions (urban car service $100B; all car service $175B; logistics $230B; mobility services $310B), maps share to a named mechanism (no network effects 5%; weak local 10%; strong local 15%; weak global 25%; strong global 40%), ties the revenue slice to competitive advantage (5–20%) and margin to the cost model (independent contractor 40%; partial employee 25%; full employee 15%) ([Damodaran, Sept 2015 deck](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/blog/UberSept2015.pdf)). That ladder is the template for stating which market you are sizing and why a share assumption is what it is, which is precisely what "why 40%?" demands. Ratio benchmarks serve the same purpose for a quick cross-check: Lightspeed's Sebastian Duesterhoeft uses security at roughly 7–10% of infrastructure cost, cloud security at 3–5% of cloud spend, and about $20–25 on the rest of the modern data stack for every $100 spent on Snowflake ([Lightspeed TAM Masterclass](https://lsvp.com/stories/a-total-addressable-market-tam-masterclass/)).

Analyst reports are a different matter. Duesterhoeft calls pasting a Gartner or IDC figure into a pitch "a big red flag," because founders "often don't know what those numbers include or exclude, or how the firms estimated them" ([Lightspeed](https://lsvp.com/stories/a-total-addressable-market-tam-masterclass/)); Index's Jahanvi Sardana says over-reliance on industry reports "can suggest the founder hasn't deeply thought through the market" ([TechCrunch, Sardana](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/25/index-ventures-jahanvi-sardana-shares-the-truth-about-tam-and-what-founders-should-focus-on-instead/)). The documented reasons go beyond snobbery. A former IDC employee's account, relayed by Engadget, says IDC "prioritized keeping growth rates consistent over accuracy, with the 'others' category serving as a balancing plug," and in one quarter IDC and Gartner estimated a decline in US Mac sales while Apple reported strong double-digit growth ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2014-07-31-why-idc-sales-estimates-are-complete-bs.html)). High-volume publishers (Grand View, MarketsandMarkets, Technavio, Mordor) sell $1,500–$5,000 reports whose methods are undisclosed and whose errors are documented: a Technavio security-camera release containing a sentence about haircare products, reports omitting the largest vendors, and a MarketsandMarkets blockchain forecast of $23.3B by 2023 against three other firms' $7.5B, $12.4B and $17.46B ([IPVM](https://ipvm.com/reports/5314); [Searchfunder](https://searchfunder.com/post/cagr-the-cynical-joke-market-research-firms-play-at-the-expense-of-gullible-executives)). Statista frequently layers an upstream CAGR onto a base rather than estimating independently ("calculated by Statista using a CAGR published by the source") ([ecommerceDB](https://ecommercedb.com/insights/chart/11285)). Their legitimate use follows directly: as one of the top-down cross-checks, recorded with publisher tier, stated definition, year and the bottom-up factor it is being compared against; for vendor-share order where a tracker's panel is strong; and as a wide-range base rate for growth, never as the point estimate.

## Reconcile the two routes by naming the one assumption that closes the gap

Every practitioner source treats a second, structurally independent route as mandatory and treats divergence as information. Triangulation is defined as using top-down "as a cross-reference to validate the results obtained through the bottom-up method" ([PrepLounge](https://www.preplounge.com/consulting-forum/top-down-vs-bottom-up-estimations-17115)); Monetizely's phrasing is "The most credible TAM analyses use at least two different methodologies to validate findings" ([Monetizely](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/total-addressable-market-tam-the-north-star-metric-for-strategic-growth)); Reach Capital's Jomayra Herrera: "You want to do bottom-up, you want to do top-down, [and] you want to do a ton of market research to triangulate" ([TechCrunch, 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/10/06/how-founders-should-approach-tam-when-venture-capital-is-scarce/)). The most important corrective comes from Stanford Biodesign's worked example: "When top-down and bottom-up market estimates vary substantially, it's not a cause for concern! More than anything, it's a healthy 'reality check'," because the bottom-up figure usually reflects today's solution space while the top-down captures the total opportunity for a better solution ([Stanford Biodesign](https://biodesignguide.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Top-Down-and-Bottom-Up-Market-Sizing-Example.pdf)).

There is no authoritative published tolerance. Coaching material says two structures landing "within about 15%" is good and within 25% of reality is excellent ([StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/?p=37611)); a slide-template guide says a modest gap "such as 15%" builds confidence because it shows triangulation rather than cherry-picking ([Deckary](https://deckary.com/blog/market-sizing-template)); a market-sizing skill file says to reconcile within 20% and revisit assumptions beyond that ([agentskillsfinder](https://agentskillsfinder.com/skills/market-sizing)); PSG says "in the same ballpark" is reasonable and "radically different" signals an error ([Fusion VC](https://blog.fusion-vc.com/p/masterclass-market-sizing)). Synthesising these, the working rule I recommend for a method doc, clearly labelled as a synthesis rather than a standard, is: for a mature, well-defined market the two routes should agree within roughly ±30% (a factor of about 1.3); for a new category a gap up to a factor of 2–3 is common and acceptable only if it is explained by a named definitional difference (served spend vs total opportunity, TAM vs SAM, units vs revenue, today's price vs post-elasticity price); beyond a factor of about 3 one route is almost certainly sizing a different thing and the market definition, not the arithmetic, must be fixed first.

The reconciliation move is always the same and an agent can run it mechanically: lay the two trees side by side, find the single leaf whose change closes most of the gap, and decide which route's version of that leaf is better evidenced. Fusion VC's advice to stress-test with a sensitivity analysis to find "the range in which the opportunity still holds" is the same operation ([Fusion VC](https://blog.fusion-vc.com/p/masterclass-market-sizing)). The sell-side version of this is the implied-share test, which converts a TAM dispute into a falsifiable claim: New Constructs, disagreeing with Snowflake's $81B management TAM, combined two third-party forecasts into a $66.1B 2027 TAM and showed the IPO price implied Snowflake would need ~46% of it ($30.3B revenue) against under 1% at the time, which was implausible next to Microsoft, Amazon, Google and Oracle ([New Constructs](https://www.newconstructs.com/?p=64519)). Damodaran's 2014 break-even did the same in reverse: a $17B Uber required either a market three times his estimate or a share above 20% ([Damodaran, June 2014](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/06/a-disruptive-cab-ride-to-riches-uber.html)).

Two further tests belong in any investor-grade sizing. The first is Damodaran's 3P test, which forces the author to say which tier each number sits in: "IT IS POSSIBLE" corresponds to TAM Overreach ("Count every conceivable customer, now or in the future"), which he rates as impossible to assess; "IT IS PLAUSIBLE" corresponds to Expanded TAM ("Start with existing market reach, but expand to include new customers drawn in both product/service changes and market trends"); "IT IS PROBABLE" corresponds to Constrained TAM ("Given product/service characteristics, count only customers that you can reach today") ([Damodaran, 2019 deck](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/blog/PelotonIPO.pdf)). A sizing that shows all three tiers with the evidence that moves a customer from one to the next is, by construction, both bottom-up (constrained) and top-down (overreach) with the reconciliation visible in between. The second is the aggregation test from Cornell and Damodaran's "Big Market Delusion": at 2015 prices, all public online-advertising companies together implied about $523B of 2025 revenue against an estimated 2025 online-ad market of about $466B, meaning the group as a whole was overpriced relative to the market whatever any one company's TAM slide said ([Damodaran, Dec 2019](https://aswathdamodaran.substack.com/p/the-market-is-huge-revisiting-the-19-12-30)). The paper's thesis is that "Big markets draw in entrepreneurs and VCs, overconfident that they can conquer these markets, which leads to a collective overpricing and an inevitable correction" ([CFA Institute, FAJ](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2020/0015198x-2020-1730655)); the operational implication is that any sizing should list the competitors and ask what share each must take for all their plans to be true. Lightspeed's fund-return test is the VC-side mirror: work backwards from the exit a fund needs (a $500M fund at 20% ownership needs a $2.5B outcome) to the revenue that implies at a plausible multiple, and then to the market a category leader's share (around 20% for horizontal SaaS) requires ([Lightspeed](https://lsvp.com/stories/a-total-addressable-market-tam-masterclass/)).

## The Uber debate rehearses every failure mode and its remedy

In June 2014 Uber raised at a $17B valuation and Aswath Damodaran tested it top-down. His chain was explicit and tabulated: a global taxi-and-limo market of "$100 billion currently, expected to grow 6% a year for next ten years," built from Tokyo $20–25B, the UK $14B, the US $11B and an estimated $50B for the rest of the world; Uber's share rising to 10% over ten years, which he called "at the optimistic end of the spectrum"; a 20% revenue slice of gross receipts; a 40% steady-state operating margin; a cost of capital starting at 12%; and a 10% failure probability, giving $6,595M of operating assets and an expected value of about $5.9B, against which $17B looked "improbable" ([Damodaran, June 2014](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/06/a-disruptive-cab-ride-to-riches-uber.html); [Damodaran, Sept 2015 deck](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/blog/UberSept2015.pdf)). Two features of this build are instructive. Half of the $100B anchor was itself an unsourced plug ($50B rest-of-world), so the number's precision outran its evidence; and his own break-even already located the two levers that would decide the argument, market size and share. The method is canonical top-down: external market × growth × share × take rate × margin, discounted.

Bill Gurley's reply a month later accepted the arithmetic and rejected the frame. "The past can be a poor guide for the future if the future offering is materially different than the past," and "This cannot be yesterday's market" ([Gurley](https://abovethecrowd.com/2014/07/11/how-to-miss-by-a-mile-an-alternative-look-at-ubers-potential-market-size/)). His rebuild had four parts. First, evidence from the first liquid city: Travis Kalanick had put historical San Francisco taxi and limo spend at about $120M and Uber's SF revenue at "a healthy multiple bigger than that," and heavy users' Uber spend "somewhere north of three times as large" as their prior taxi spend. Second, mechanism: high price elasticity ("lowering the price of car-for-hire transportation will increase the usage") and three local network effects (pick-up times, coverage density, utilisation). Third, new use cases: suburbs, rental-car replacement (a $27B US market), nights out, transporting children and elderly parents, supplementing transit. Fourth, substitution of a much larger spend pool: roughly one billion cars worldwide at an AAA ownership cost of $9,000 cut to $6,000 "for conservatism," about $6 trillion a year, of which 2.5–12.5% capture gives $150–750B. Adding a car-for-hire market expanded 3–6× over the historic base, he reached a TAM of roughly $450B to $1.3T and showed that his bearish scenario required Uber to take about 56% of it and his likely scenario about 20% ([Gurley](https://abovethecrowd.com/2014/07/11/how-to-miss-by-a-mile-an-alternative-look-at-ubers-potential-market-size/)). The honest reading is that Gurley's build is bottom-up in two senses, starting from measured per-city behaviour and building a population × unit cost × capture chain, but the 2.5–12.5% capture rates had no empirical anchor, so the top of his range was as much a plug as Damodaran's rest-of-world. The San Francisco ratio is the strongest single piece of evidence in the exchange because it is a measurement, not a forecast.

Damodaran's response within the week introduced the vocabulary that still governs these arguments: "not everything that is possible is plausible," and not every plausible outcome becomes probable. He accepted market expansion as plausible-to-probable pending test-market data, attached an option value of only $2–3B to car-ownership substitution because it was distant and unformed, and insisted that Gurley's three network effects were local, implying city-by-city fights with different winners ("Uber in San Francisco, Lyft in Chicago, a new firm in London") ([Damodaran, July 2014](https://aswathdamodaran.substack.com/p/possible-plausible-and-probable-big-14-07-16)). Re-running his model on Gurley's narrative ($300B market, 40% share, 20% slice) gave $53.4B. By September 2015, citing a leaked report that Uber had made the SF market "three to four times larger" than the original taxi market and gross billings heading past $10B, he moved his inputs to a $230B "logistics" market growing 10.4% a year, 25% share, a slice fading from 20% to 15%, a 25% margin under a "partial employee" cost model, and zero failure probability, for a value of $23.4B, writing "I will remain grateful to Bill Gurley for a post where he took me to task for having too narrow a vision of Uber's business model" ([Damodaran, Oct 2015](https://aswathdamodaran.substack.com/p/on-the-uber-rollercoaster-narrative-15-10-12); [Sept 2015 deck](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/blog/UberSept2015.pdf)). The shape of that correction is the classic one for a top-down model: market up 2.3×, share up 2.5×, but take rate and margin down, netting about 4× on value.

Uber's own 2019 S-1 then demonstrated the opposite failure. Its "Personal Mobility TAM consists of 11.9 trillion miles per year, representing an estimated $5.7 trillion market opportunity in 175 countries," comprising all passenger-vehicle miles and all public-transport miles on earth including countries Uber had not entered, with a SAM of $2.5T in 57 countries limited to trips under 30 miles; the miles were estimated by multiplying cars per country by miles per car, and Uber's 26B platform miles represented "less than 1% penetration of our near-term SAM" ([Uber S-1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1543151/000119312519103850/d647752ds1.htm)). Ben Thompson noted that $5.7T is about 7% of gross world product and called "small share of a very large market" the most clichéd startup argument ([Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2019/uber-questions/)); Damodaran's slide was titled "The Business Spin! Personal Mobility? Really?" and he abandoned a top-down approach for Uber altogether in favour of a rider-based valuation because "The uncertainty about the total accessible market ... makes me uneasy with my top down valuation" ([Damodaran, 2019 IPO deck](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/blog/UberIPO.pdf)). A market so large that the share needed looks trivial removes the discipline the share assumption exists to provide, which is the Big Market Delusion in one filing.

Because Damodaran's base year was 2014 and his "year 10" was 2024, hindsight is direct.

| Quantity (2024 unless noted) | Damodaran, June 2014 | Gurley, July 2014 | Actual |
|---|---|---|---|

The scorecard yields five transferable lessons. On market size, the frame beat the arithmetic by an order of magnitude: Uber's 2024 bookings were about nine times Damodaran's projection for Uber and roughly as large as his projection for the entire 2024 global taxi market, so "the product expands the market" was not merely true but dominant. Gurley's upper magnitudes remain unproven a decade on, since FY2025 bookings of $193B are 15–43% of his range and the car-ownership tranche is not separately observable, which is the appropriate humility for any capture-rate plug. Damodaran was right about mechanism: network effects proved regional, the 20% take rate came under pressure, and margins were far below 40%, with operating losses until 2023 and an FY2025 operating margin near 11% of revenue, so a sizing that is right about the market can still be wrong about the economics, and vice versa. When testing the market-size claim, compare gross bookings rather than revenue, because reported revenue mixes take-rate changes and accounting bases (Uber's revenue-to-bookings ratio moved from 22.6% in 2018 to roughly 27% in 2025) ([Uber S-1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1543151/000119312519103850/d647752ds1.htm); [Uber FY2025 results](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260204947622/en/Uber-Announces-Results-for-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2025)). And the largest hindsight lesson is not top-down versus bottom-up at all: neither man modelled food delivery, which now books nearly as much as rides, so adjacent-market optionality that both sides treated as a footnote became half the company. The method implication is to size adjacencies as separate, explicitly probability-weighted options with their own trees, not to fold them into the core TAM and not to ignore them.

Two sentiments from the investor roundups should temper how much an agent lets a user polish the number. Felicis's Aydin Senkut: "With TAM, it is almost guaranteed you're going to be wrong," and "The plan doesn't have to be accurate: The plan has to be directionally correct"; Lux's Deena Shakir: "We can tell if a founder has just done some calculations and got excited about a TAM" ([TechCrunch, 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/11/04/what-investors-really-think-about-the-tam-slide-in-your-pitch-deck/)). Sardana's three buckets are the right first question for a new category: a "known market" (show "why you're building a better toothbrush"), an "emerging market" (non-alcoholic beer), or an "invisible market" ("the biggest trap", "a dark art"), with the reminder that Index passed on Airbnb because the TAM looked too small ([TechCrunch, Sardana](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/25/index-ventures-jahanvi-sardana-shares-the-truth-about-tam-and-what-founders-should-focus-on-instead/)). The investor-side protocol for an invisible market, assembled from Gurley, Pear, Sardana and Shakir, is to name the bucket, size the wedge bottom-up with real counts and an interview-validated price, show an early-cohort ratio over the legacy market, size the adjacent spend pools being substituted under explicit penetration scenarios, and present the range with the share implied for a venture outcome.

## Counts and prices come from a tiered source stack with known lags

A bottom-up tree needs three kinds of leaf (a count, a price, a frequency or penetration), and the source for each should be recorded with its tier, grain, reference date and exclusions. For the United States the Census Bureau's business statistics plus BLS form a complete, free, NAICS- and SOC-coded counting stack; Europe, the UK, Canada and the OECD have equivalents.

The grain distinction matters: SUSB counts firms and CBP counts establishments, so the firm count is the denominator for "companies that could buy" and the establishment count for "sites to deploy to." The lags are systematic enough to be a register field: a count sourced from SUSB in 2026 describes 2023, and the growth adjustment to today deserves its own range.

Prices and spend have a parallel hierarchy, from transaction data through filings and pricing pages down to review sites. SEC EDGAR's XBRL APIs return "all the company concepts data for a company into a single API call," require no key, and update within a minute of filing ([SEC EDGAR APIs](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces)). Backing ARPU or ACV out of a 10-K is period-end ARR ÷ period-end customers, but only after normalising definitions that filings explicitly warn are not comparable: SailPoint reports customers by ARR band (under $250K, over $250K, over $1M) and includes maintenance and term licences in ARR; Appgate counts a customer as "a distinct organization that has entered into a distinct agreement"; HashiCorp rolls divisions into one customer and bases consumption ARR on the last three months of spend ([SailPoint 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2030781/000203078126000003/sail-20260131.htm); [Appgate 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1353538/000162828023010179/apgt-20221231.htm); [HashiCorp 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1720671/000162828024012350/hcp-20240131.htm)). Where only bands are disclosed, bracket the tier ACV rather than computing a mean. For transaction-level evidence, Vendr's price-estimate tool draws on "over 30,000 contracts," and its public series shows how volatile ACV is (a three-year low of $62k in Q2 2023, $89k the next quarter, a 2023 blended $87k), which is a reason to carry a range rather than a point ([Vendr 2023 report](https://www.vendr.com/insights/saas-trends-report-2023); [Vendr docs](https://developers.vendr.com/docs/solution/price-estimate)). Government buyers' prices are visible at award level in USAspending/FPDS, with the caveat that GAO found "missing information, impossibly large subaward amounts, and likely duplicative records" ([GAO-20-75](https://gao.gov/assets/gao-20-75.pdf)). Review sites are the weakest tier: "only 4% of G2 product profiles explicitly list prices as of March 2025," and listings contain obvious data-entry errors such as a starting price of $18,000 per user per month ([G2 Learn](https://learn.g2.com/software-pricing-transparency); [Capterra listing](https://capterra.com/p/239087/G2-Track)).

When no price exists because the market is new, the price leaf comes from willingness-to-pay research, and the pricing literature is unambiguous that this happens before building: "It is essential to have the 'willingness to pay' talk early in the product development process," with the conversation ordered problem → solution → value → price and the explicit trap that "an average of $60 when customers cluster at $20 and $100" hides the segmentation the sizing needs ([Brikman, notes on Monetizing Innovation](https://www.ybrikman.com/blog/2023/04/01/monetizing-innovation/)). Van Westendorp's four questions (too cheap to trust, a bargain, getting expensive, too expensive to consider) define an acceptable range without a reference price and suit "novel offerings where market price expectations aren't well-established"; Gabor-Granger's iterative "Would you buy at X?" produces a demand curve and suits products whose packaging is final, with a recommended sample of at least 100 and the limitation that respondents judge the product "in isolation" ([Monetizely, PSM vs Gabor-Granger](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/van-westendorp-vs-gabor-granger-for-saas-which-pricing-methodology-to-choose); [IntoTheMinds, Gabor-Granger](https://www.intotheminds.com/blog/en/gabor-granger/)). That split lines up with Blank's market types: Van Westendorp for new and resegmented markets, Gabor-Granger for existing ones. The cheapest defensible price evidence of all is Hubbard's Rule of Five: five random customer quotes or contracts bracket the population median with 93.75% probability, because P(all five on one side of the median) = 2 × 0.5⁵ = 0.0625 ([DADM FAQ](https://rww.quarto.pub/dadm-faq/posts/rule-of-five/index.html)). Lux's Shakir recommends exactly this kind of one-on-one interview to gather pricing and behaviour data for new markets ([TechCrunch, 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/11/04/what-investors-really-think-about-the-tam-slide-in-your-pitch-deck/)).

## Carry ranges through the tree, then write down what would change your mind

Multiplying best guesses through a five-leaf tree produces a point estimate whose implied precision nobody can defend, and the research on judgmental intervals shows how badly intuition understates uncertainty. McKenzie and colleagues summarise the literature: "If people were well calibrated, 90% of their 90% confidence intervals would contain the true value. However, true values typically fall within such intervals between 30% and 60% of the time," and professionals in their own domains manage 40–60% ([McKenzie et al. 2008](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~cmckenzie/McKenzieetal2008OBHDP.pdf)). A 2024 study of 285 participants found 90% ranges captured the truth only 30–35% of the time, "indicating gross overprecision," and Fermi-style prompting reduced it only slightly ([Scientific Reports 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10904748/)). The default assumption for any leaf a user types is therefore that its range is two to three times too narrow.

Hubbard's calibration tools are the fix and they fit an interactive agent well. The equivalent-bet test compares the user's 90% range against a wheel that pays 90% of the time: "if you would rather spin the wheel than rely on your range, your range is too narrow," and you widen until the two bets feel equal ([LessWrong, calibration](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5XBv2onWGRKFtAEMN/link-how-to-calibrate-your-confidence-intervals)). Other prompts that work: test each bound separately ("is there a 95% chance the answer is above my lower bound?"), "find bounds that are absurd, then move inward," and "Identify two pros and two cons for the range that you have given" ([The Uncertainty Project](https://www.theuncertaintyproject.org/tools/estimator-calibration)). Hubbard's claim that most people become near-perfectly calibrated within half a day of training is reported only secondhand and has no peer-reviewed replication that this research found, so a method doc should promise improvement, not perfection ([Hubbard Decision Research](https://hubbardresearch.com/calibration-training/)).

Once every leaf has a 90% interval, the tree should be run as a Monte Carlo rather than multiplied at the endpoints, which overstates the spread, or at the midpoints, which hides it; Causal's blog named the second error "the flaw of averages" ([Causal](https://causal.app/blog/forecasting-with-uncertainty)). Guesstimate is the free, open-source, browser-based tool built for exactly this: cells accept "5 to 9" as a 90% range, outputs come from 5,000 Monte Carlo samples, and public models are free and shareable, which makes a Guesstimate link a de facto auditable artefact ([Guesstimate docs](https://docs.getguesstimate.com/); [EA Forum announcement](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/Bt4nkCGHKBkDk97mn/guesstimate-an-app-for-making-decisions-with-confidence)). Squiggle, from the same author, is a text language in which distributions are first-class values (`normal(10, 2) * uniform(0.8, 1.2)`), suited to a file in version control ([LongtermWiki, Squiggle](https://www.longtermwiki.com/knowledge-base/responses/epistemic-tools/tools/squiggle)). A plain spreadsheet with lognormal inverse-CDF draws and a data table is sufficient for a five-to-ten-leaf sizing and avoids paid add-ins such as Crystal Ball or @RISK; at least 1,000 runs is the usual rule of thumb ([MoreThanDigital](https://morethandigital.info/en/integrating-uncertainty-into-business-cases-for-better-investment-decisions/)). Low/base/high cases are the deterministic cousin of this and should be defined as the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles of each leaf so they agree with the simulation rather than being chosen by feel.

The tornado chart then answers "what should we research next." Each leaf is swung between its low and high while the others sit at base, bars are sorted by absolute swing, and the result's limitation, that it cannot capture interactions, is why the probabilistic run is kept alongside it ([MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/deterministic-sensitivity-tornado/)). Hubbard's measurement-inversion finding pairs with it: "the economic value of measuring a variable is usually inversely proportional to how much measurement attention it usually gets," so the widest bar is usually the least-researched leaf and the next interview, filing or dataset should go there ([LessWrong summary of Hubbard](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ybYBCK9D7MZCcdArB/how-to-measure-anything)). Superforecasting supplies the remaining habits: start from the outside view ("How often do things of this sort happen in situations of this sort?") before adjusting for the inside view, since comparison-class training correlated with accuracy in the Good Judgment Project; update in small increments; and "Acknowledge the strongest counterargument to your view, and decide in advance what evidence would change your mind" ([Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/ten-commandments-for-superforecasters/); [AI Impacts](https://aiimpacts.org/?p=1283)).

No widely adopted public assumptions-register template for market sizing exists; the design that follows synthesises the practitioner guidance that each input should keep "sourced facts, inferred estimates, and judgment calls" distinct and "make the source path visible," plus a three-tier confidence convention (HIGH: multiple corroborating recent sources; MEDIUM: single authoritative source one to two years old; LOW: extrapolated or old) ([OpenAI market-sizing skill, mirror](https://mcpservers.org/agent-skills/openai/market-sizing); [Orchestkit skill](https://skillselion.com/skills/yonatangross/orchestkit/market-sizing)). Each leaf's row should carry: an ID and definition with unit, segment and geography; low/base/high as 5th/50th/95th percentiles; source URL, publisher and tier (official statistic / filing / transaction data / vendor estimate / analyst report / judgment); reference date of the data and date accessed; grain (NAICS/SOC/NACE code, size band) and definitional notes (which ARR definition, installs versus users); confidence tier with reason; the counter-case and the evidence that would move it; two pros and two cons for the range; tornado rank; and the next validation step with an owner. For analyst figures the row must also say whether the methodology is disclosed.

Projecting a TAM into a year-by-year path is the last piece, and the Bass model is the standard tool: adoption follows dF/dt = (1 − F)(p + qF) with m the market potential, p the coefficient of innovation (typically 0.01–0.03 per year) and q the coefficient of imitation (typically 0.3–0.5, mean about 0.38), and the peak-sales year is t* = ln(q/p)/(p + q), about six years at the mean values ([Wikipedia, Bass model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_diffusion_model)). With no sales history, p and q are borrowed from three to five named analogs and fitted only once at least five periods of actuals exist ([Forecast Pro](https://forecastpro.com/Resource-Center/v14/Documentation/pages/bass-diffusion.html)). The simplest defensible PM version is to set m to the constrained TAM in units, run fast/base/slow cases on q (word of mouth is the dominant uncertainty), and resist updating m on the first year's actuals, since early sales are dominated by p·m and say little about the ceiling. The known pitfall is that enthusiasts "often overestimate short-term growth and, as a result, underestimate long-term growth" ([Marketing Study Guide](https://www.marketingstudyguide.com/using-the-bass-forecasting-model/)), which is the quantitative form of Blank's warning that companies in new markets "who hire and execute like they're in an Existing Market burn through their cash" ([Steve Blank, 2010](https://steveblank.com/2010/02/16/death-by-revenue-plan/)).

## A guided path from "no idea" to investor-grade, and the sources to cite

An agent guiding a novice should run the following sequence, each step of which is backed by the sources above. Start by defining the market as a group of job executors plus the job, in words that contain no product name and no job title, and classify the market type (existing, resegmented, new or clone) and Sardana's bucket (known, emerging, invisible), because those two labels decide which structures, price methods and adoption curves apply ([Steve Blank, 2021](https://steveblank.com/2021/11/04/market-definition-its-the-front-end-of-customer-discovery/); [TechCrunch, Sardana](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/25/index-ventures-jahanvi-sardana-shares-the-truth-about-tam-and-what-founders-should-focus-on-instead/)). Next, write the ICP as the tiers of accounts (or people) that could buy, with the unit the price will be charged in, and count each tier from an official source first (SUSB, OEWS, ACS, Eurostat SBS) and a vendor cut second, recording reference year and exclusions. Then establish the price leaf from the strongest available tier (early deals or contracts; filings' ARR ÷ customers with definitions normalised; transaction benchmarks; pricing pages; then WTP research with Van Westendorp or Gabor-Granger and a Rule-of-Five sample), and for new markets quantify the job's current cost (workaround spend, ownership cost, hours × loaded rate) as the value ceiling the price must sit well below. Build the bottom-up tree as Σ tiers × price, give every leaf a calibrated 90% range using the equivalent-bet test, and run the Monte Carlo.

Only then build the top-down: pick a structure from the menu, state the nested market definitions and the tier (constrained, expanded, overreach) each sits in, justify every leaf in one line, round aggressively, and sanity-check with a per-capita restatement and a ratio benchmark; add any analyst figure as a third, labelled cross-check. Reconcile the routes by identifying the one leaf that closes most of the gap, deciding which route evidences it better, and writing the explanation into the register; if the gap exceeds a factor of three, fix the market definition before touching the numbers. Convert the sizing into falsifiable claims with the implied-share test against the plan, the $100M ladder against the funnel, the fund-return test against the investor's needs, and the aggregation test against named competitors. For markets the product will expand, present constrained and expanded tiers with a measured early-cohort ratio where one exists, and size adjacencies as separate probability-weighted trees. Project with Bass or an analog S-curve, a hockey stick for new markets, and three cases on q. Finally, publish the tornado, the register with counter-cases and "what would change my mind," and the two-route summary on one page, since "two candidates can reach different answers and both pass" when the reasoning is visible ([Leland](https://www.joinleland.com/library/a/the-expert-guide-to-market-sizing-questions-with-examples)).

## Conclusion

The deepest pattern in this material is that every famous disagreement about a market size (Gurley versus Damodaran, New Constructs versus Snowflake, real-estate analysts versus WeWork) was a disagreement about market definition, not arithmetic, and that the sources on both sides agree on the remedy: say which market you are counting, show the count and the price, and let the reader move between the constrained, expanded and overreach tiers with the evidence that justifies each step. A method that forces those tiers into the open turns the top-down versus bottom-up question from a stylistic choice into a reconciliation, with the gap between the two routes becoming a measured quantity that names the next thing to go and learn. The Uber hindsight sharpens the point further: being right about the market and wrong about the economics, or vice versa, are both common, and the half of Uber that is delivery shows that adjacencies deserve their own probability-weighted trees rather than a footnote or a trillion-dollar merger into the core.

For the PM building an agent, the practical implication is that credibility is a property of the artefact, not the number. An output that carries a register with sources, dates, grain, calibrated ranges and counter-cases, a Monte Carlo with a tornado, a two-route summary with the reconciling assumption named, and implied-share and aggregation tests against the plan will survive a partner meeting even when its central estimate is later proven wrong, which the investors quoted here say it almost certainly will be. The agent's job is less to compute a TAM than to refuse to let any leaf exist without a justification, a range and a way to be proven wrong.
