# The analyst / consultant craft of market sizing: structure, estimation, triangulation, standards

Scope: how sell-side equity analysts, management consultants (case-method tradition) and corporate strategists build a market sizing from nothing, the estimation techniques they borrow from Fermi/"guesstimation" practice, how they reconcile independent routes, the public critiques of IPO TAM claims, and the simplest defensible diffusion model for a PM. Research date: 2026-10-09. Roughly 30 web searches/fetches; several primary pages (Management Consulted, MIT Press, OAPEN) returned 403/timeouts and are flagged below.

## KQ1. Consulting case-method market sizing: the standard decomposition, MECE/issue trees, explicit rounded assumptions, second-route sanity checks, and which public guides state them best

### Takeaway
The consulting method is a fixed six-beat routine (clarify → structure a 3–5 driver multiplicative tree → state rounded, justified assumptions → calculate → sanity-check against a benchmark or a second structure → interpret), with MECE decomposition (Barbara Minto, McKinsey, late 1960s) as the structural rule. The best freely available, explicit statement of the routine found in this research is StrategyCase's 2023/2026 guide by an ex-McKinsey consultant; Cheng's and Cosentino's own texts are paywalled books and only secondary summaries were retrievable.

### Cited Findings
- **The six-step routine (explicit, free, ex-McKinsey author).** StrategyCase ("Market Sizing Questions: 25 Examples With Worked Answers", Dr. Florian Smeritschnig, former McKinsey Senior Consultant; first published 31 Jan 2023, page marked updated 24 Sep 2026) lays out: (1) Clarify unit, geography, time period and restate the objective; (2) Structure: "break the problem into three to five drivers that multiply together" and say the structure aloud before calculating; (3) Assume: "simple, rounded assumptions", each justified "with a benchmark, personal behavior, or basic logic"; (4) Calculate step by step without "false precision"; (5) Sanity-check: "compare with a known benchmark, triangulate with a second structure, or check the implied per-unit figure"; (6) Interpret the number for the business, including the key sensitivity — [StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/?p=37611)
- **Canonical formulas / structures.** The same guide catalogues the standard trees: top-down "Population × % target × usage × price" (consumer markets with a clean starting population); bottom-up "Units × usage per unit × price"; capacity "Capacity per unit × units × utilization" (bottleneck-limited supply); installed-base replacement "Units in use ÷ years each lasts, plus growth" (durables in mature markets); life-event flow "Population × share affected ÷ years per lifetime" (one-time events such as weddings); and supply-from-demand "Total demand ÷ output per unit". It notes the same product can need different structures: a national pizza market is top-down, a single restaurant is better built from kitchen capacity — [StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/?p=37611)
- **Rounding and justification rules, verbatim.** "Round aggressively: 10%, 25%, 50% are far easier to work with than 12.7%." "Never pull a number from thin air without a one-line justification." "A number you have not sanity-checked is a liability." — [StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/?p=37611)
- **Accuracy standard the case tradition applies.** The guide states that an answer "within the right order of magnitude generally passes, and landing within about 25% of reality is excellent." Its own worked estimates vs published actuals: Italian weddings ~220k vs 173,272 (ISTAT 2024, ~27% high); new German cars ~2.9M vs 2.82M (KBA 2024, ~3%); German fuel stations ~11,000 vs 14,018 (bft 2025, ~21% low); Heathrow passengers ~80M vs 83.9M (2024, ~5%); US coffee-shop cups/day ~20M with top-down and bottom-up "within about 15%" — [StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/?p=37611)
- **Common failure modes listed.** Memorising a rigid framework instead of reasoning; choosing a structure whose inputs you cannot defend; overcomplicated math; unrealistic assumptions; going silent; sizing the wrong thing (whole market when the client asked for its own revenue); forgetting a wedding involves two people — [StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/?p=37611)
- **Sanity check by per-capita restatement.** Hacking the Case Interview: sanity-checking compares the answer to "a reference point you already trust"; e.g., restate 300 piano tuners in a city as "one tuner per 28,000 people" and ask whether that is plausible — [Hacking the Case Interview](https://www.hackingthecaseinterview.com/pages/estimation-techniques-case-interview); CaseBasix defines sanity checks as "quick reality checks candidates use to confirm that numbers, assumptions, and conclusions make business sense before communicating them" — [CaseBasix](https://www.casebasix.com/pages/sanity-checks-case-interviews)
- **Cosentino (Case in Point), via a university guide crediting him.** Market sizing answers "should rest on logic and assumptions, which can be wrong as long as they are reasonable"; question families are population-based (break down by generation), household (break down by income level), worldwide, and "preposterous" questions; worked example: US coffee consumption = population split by generation × share drinking coffee per generation × daily cups by lifestyle — [Colorado College Career Center case guide (credits Cosentino)](https://www.coloradocollege.edu/offices/careercenter/documents/guides/caseinterviewguide). A Pitt MBA "Case in Point Workshop" deck (2016) lists the opening sequence: summarize the question, verify objectives, ask clarifying questions, lay out your structure — [Pitt Katz](https://inet.katz.pitt.edu:443/studentnet/mba/transition/MBA%20Transition%20Module%20Powerpoint%20Presentations/2016%20-%20FT%20Orientation%20-%20Friday%20August%2019%20-%20Case%20in%20Point%20Workshop.pdf)
- **Victor Cheng.** Cheng is an ex-McKinsey consultant and author of *Case Interview Secrets*; the IUCG/UMass "Mastering the Case Interview Principles" guide recommends his book alongside Cosentino's as standard prep reading — [UMass/IUCG guide](https://cdn.sbspathways.umass.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/105/2020/11/Mastering-the-Case-Interview-Principles-by-IUCG.pdf). No page on caseinterview.com with his market-sizing steps was retrievable via search; only an eFinancialCareers piece says estimation questions are "about your ability [to] make fair assumptions, rationalize those educated guesses and apply mathematical skills" with tips "courtesy of Victor Cheng at CaseInterview.com" — [eFinancialCareers 2014](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2014/02/can-you-conquer-the-hardest-strategy-questions-asked-in-finance-interviews)
- **MECE: origin and definition.** MECE ("mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive") groups items into subsets that do not overlap and together cover the whole set; developed by Barbara Minto at McKinsey in the late 1960s and underlying her Pyramid Principle; Minto credits the idea to Aristotle. Criticisms: it does not exclude superfluous items, forcing answers (rather than categories) to be MECE can be unnecessarily restrictive, and it precludes redundancy that is sometimes desirable. Secondary references listed: Minto's McKinsey Alumni Center interview "MECE: I invented it, so I get to say how to pronounce it"; Chevallier, *Strategic Thinking in Complex Problem Solving* (OUP 2016), p.78 — [Wikipedia: MECE principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MECE_principle)
- **Two routes on one page as a presentation norm.** A slide-template guide argues that showing both top-down and bottom-up with a modest gap "such as 15%" builds confidence because it shows triangulation rather than cherry-picking — [Deckary](https://deckary.com/blog/market-sizing-template)
- **Interviewers judge reasoning, not the number.** "Two candidates can reach different answers and both pass"; try both approaches on one prompt, then ask which assumptions drive the answer most and whether the result passes a basic reality check — [Leland guide](https://www.joinleland.com/library/a/the-expert-guide-to-market-sizing-questions-with-examples); [CaseStar](https://www.casestar.io/guides/market-sizing)

### Inferences
- The consulting standard is procedural, not numerical: a sizing is "defensible" when (a) the tree is MECE with 3–5 multiplicative drivers, (b) every leaf has a one-line justification, (c) numbers are rounded to tractable values, (d) a second route or known benchmark lands within tens of percent, and (e) the key sensitivity is named. These five properties can be turned directly into a checklist for a PM method doc.
- "Within an order of magnitude passes; within ~25% is excellent" (StrategyCase) is the clearest published accuracy yardstick from the case tradition found in this research; it is a coaching standard, not an industry standard.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve Victor Cheng's or Marc Cosentino's own text (both are commercial books; search returned only low-quality PDF mirrors and secondary summaries). Management Consulted's market-sizing page returned HTTP 403.
- No McKinsey-authored public piece specifically on market sizing was found; MECE is documented only via secondary sources (Wikipedia, Minto interview). Public consulting-club casebooks (Wharton/Kellogg/MIT) were not retrieved.

## KQ2. Fermi estimation / guesstimation: Weinstein & Adam, Mahajan, Hubbard (calibrated 90% intervals, decomposition, Rule of Five) and the evidence that decomposition helps

### Takeaway
The toolkit is: round to powers of ten, bound each unknown with an "absurdly low / absurdly high" pair and take the geometric mean, decompose into quantities you know better than the target, give 90% intervals and calibrate them (Hubbard), and use tiny samples (Rule of Five: 5 random draws bracket the median with 93.75% probability). The evidence that decomposition improves accuracy is real but conditional: it helps most for extreme, highly uncertain targets (MacGregor & Armstrong 1994) and for groups (Barrera-Lemarchand 2026), can hurt when the sub-judgments are unfamiliar (Goodwin & Wright 1993), and people's 90% ranges are badly overprecise without training (hit rates 30–41% in two samples).

### Cited Findings
- **Weinstein & Adam, *Guesstimation* (Princeton UP, 2008).** Authors are physics (Weinstein) and mathematics (Adam) professors at Old Dominion University. Method: "order-of-magnitude estimation... rounded to the nearest power of ten", using "plausible assumptions and elementary arithmetic", breaking a complex problem into "simpler, more manageable ones"; "there can be many paths to the right answer." A *Choice* review notes the early chapters explain why the geometric mean is preferred over the arithmetic mean when combining bounds — [Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691129495/guesstimation)
- **Mahajan, *Street-Fighting Mathematics* (MIT Press, 2010, ISBN 9780262514293), open access.** Subtitle "The Art of Educated Guessing and Opportunistic Problem Solving"; six tools: dimensional analysis, easy cases, lumping, picture proofs, successive approximation, reasoning by analogy. Catalogued as open access in OAPEN and DOAB; sources conflict on whether the licence is CC BY-NC-ND or CC BY-NC-SA — [OAPEN record via UPI library](https://oalib-perpustakaan.upi.edu/Record/oapen_2024_20_500_12657_26090/Description?print=1); [TAMIU OER record](https://oer.tamiu.edu/itemRecord.php?id=106186). (MIT Press's own page returned 403 and the OAPEN handle timed out, so the direct PDF URL was not confirmed.)
- **Hubbard, *How to Measure Anything* (Wiley; 1st ed. 2007, 2nd 2010, 3rd 2014).** Calibration training: trainees answer general-knowledge questions with 90% ranges plus true/false questions with confidence levels; the goal is that "when you say you are 90% confident, you will be correct 90% of the time"; chapter 5 uses trivia such as the air distance LA–NY as the exercise — [LessWrong summary](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5XBv2onWGRKFtAEMN/link-how-to-calibrate-your-confidence-intervals); [Hubbard Decision Research calibration page](https://hubbardresearch.com/calibration-training/). HDR says it has calibrated "thousands of subject matter experts" but publishes no before/after figures on that page — [HDR](https://hubbardresearch.com/calibration-training/). Hubbard's claim (from his book, secondhand in sources) is that about 90% of people become near-perfectly calibrated within half a day of training; no independent peer-reviewed replication of that figure was found — [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5XBv2onWGRKFtAEMN/link-how-to-calibrate-your-confidence-intervals)
- **Calibration techniques.** The "equivalent bet" test: imagine betting on your 90% range vs a spinner that wins 90% of the time; if you prefer the spinner, widen the range. Calibration tips: focus on what you already know and "find bounds that are absurd, then move inward" — [The Uncertainty Project, estimator calibration](https://www.theuncertaintyproject.org/tools/estimator-calibration); [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5XBv2onWGRKFtAEMN/link-how-to-calibrate-your-confidence-intervals)
- **How overprecise untrained 90% intervals are.** Online quiz of 218 respondents: a stated 90% interval contained the true answer only 41% of the time, with item-level hit rates 23–75% (self-selected sample) — [Messy Matters](https://messymatters.com/calibration-results). Gomilsek, Hoffrage & Marewski (2024; 285 Prolific participants): 90% ranges captured the true value only 30–35% of the time, "indicating gross overprecision"; the Fermi conditions reduced it only slightly — [Scientific Reports 14:5014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10904748/)
- **Rule of Five (Hubbard 2010).** "The median of any population should be between the lowest and highest collected values in a sample of five" with ~94% probability. Math: each draw has p=0.5 of falling below the median; P(all five on one side) = 2 × 0.5^5 = 0.0625, so P(median inside the sample range) = 0.9375 — [DADM FAQ (Quarto)](https://rww.quarto.pub/dadm-faq/posts/rule-of-five); [TechTarget definition](https://www.techtarget.com/whatis/definition/rule-of-five-statistics)
- **Hubbard's decomposition rules (via reader notes on the cybersecurity edition).** Decompose into quantities you can estimate better than the original question; check the decomposition against a directly estimated range using simulation; Fermi decomposition plus calibrated intervals on each component, combined by Monte Carlo, yields a calibrated interval for the whole — [Goodreads reader note](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/31287491-how-to-measure-anything-in-cybersecurity-risk/80092377-g-ran-f-breivik/b844d89b-e88e-409b-ab01-65724975db42); [GreaterWrong comment](https://greaterwrong.com/posts/5XBv2onWGRKFtAEMN/link-how-to-calibrate-your-confidence-intervals/comment/8xtJLdB3uxcg2mAtc) (both are secondary/low-authority)
- **Evidence: decomposition helps for extreme, uncertain targets.** MacGregor & Armstrong, "Judgmental Decomposition: When Does It Work?" (1994): reanalysis of two earlier studies found decomposition improved accuracy for nine problems with extreme, uncertain target values and not for six whose targets were not extreme/uncertain; a new experiment (10 problems, 280 subjects, 1,078 estimates) found decomposition improved accuracy when the target was an extreme, uncertain value and "otherwise it often produced less accurate predictions" — [UPenn repository record](https://upenn-test.atmire.com/entities/publication/81519b4c-531b-450f-91f5-fc473a09d275); related earlier work: MacGregor, Lichtenstein & Slovic, "Structuring knowledge retrieval," *OBHDP* 42(3):303–323 (1988), paywalled — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v42y1988i3p303-323.html)
- **Evidence: null and negative results.** Henrion, Fischer & Mullin (1993) found decomposition did not significantly change accuracy of assessed medians or calibration, but shifted bias from under- to over-estimation of uncertain quantities; Goodwin & Wright (1993): decomposition "is not guaranteed to improve accuracy and may actually reduce it" when the decomposed judgments are more complex or less familiar, or cause fatigue; Lawrence et al. (2006 review): "Surprisingly, there has been relatively little research over the last 25 years into the value of decomposition." Armstrong (2001) distinguishes multiplicative decomposition (sales = market size × share) from additive segmentation (sales = sum of regions) — [EA Forum review of decomposition evidence](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/beRtXkMpCT39y8bPj/there-is-little-evidence-on-question-decomposition)
- **Evidence: individuals vs groups.** Gomilsek et al. (2024): a "Fermian" similarity-based strategy produced the largest error reduction on untrimmed averages (aided-Fermian Δ = 4.3 pts, d = 0.50) but the strategy main effect was not significant (F(2,279)=1.07, p=.345) and the advantage disappeared under 20% trimming — [Sci Rep 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10904748/). Barrera-Lemarchand et al. (2026, "Collective Fermi Estimation", three experiments): spontaneous decomposition rated by annotators predicts lower collective error; groups instructed to decompose outperform groups instructed to combine initial guesses; gains larger collectively than individually — [UTDT repository](https://repositorio.utdt.edu/items/721cc53d-66a4-45c8-ab56-83e928f20a97/full); a 2024 study of 130 four-person chat groups found group estimates more accurate, "especially in those groups that use the Fermi method" — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/dbk/procee/v2y2024ip1056294piii2024257id1056294piii2024257.html). An earlier individual-level test giving sub-questions found no measurable accuracy gain — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt9g34x1mk/qt9g34x1mk.pdf)

### Inferences
- Market sizes are precisely the "extreme, uncertain" targets where MacGregor & Armstrong found decomposition pays off, so the consulting instinct to decompose is supported — but only if each leaf is genuinely easier to estimate than the whole (Hubbard's rule) and familiar to the estimator (Goodwin & Wright's caveat).
- The strongest, cheapest discipline a PM can add is Hubbard-style ranges: give an absurd-low/absurd-high pair per leaf, narrow to a 90% range, and expect it to be too narrow by default (untrained hit rates 30–41%).
- Multiplying point estimates compounds error; carrying ranges (or a quick Monte Carlo) through the tree is the technically correct way to state uncertainty on the final number.

### Gaps
- Hubbard's own text on decomposition and his training results could not be retrieved; claims rest on secondary summaries. No peer-reviewed replication of "90% calibrated after half a day" was found.
- The original full texts of MacGregor & Armstrong 1994 and Henrion et al. 1993 were not read; findings are from repository abstracts and a review post. No effect sizes for the 1994 study.

## KQ3. Triangulation: reconciling top-down and bottom-up, and what divergence is acceptable

### Takeaway
Every practitioner source treats a second, structurally independent route as mandatory and treats divergence as diagnostic (trace it to the assumption or to a market-definition mismatch), but there is no authoritative published tolerance: coaching material cites 15–25% as "excellent", one skill-file heuristic says reconcile within 20%, the case tradition passes anything within an order of magnitude, and the Stanford Biodesign guide explicitly says a large top-down/bottom-up gap is "not a cause for concern" because the two measure different things (today's solution space vs. the total opportunity).

### Cited Findings
- **Triangulation defined as the cross-check.** Top-down "used as a cross-reference to validate the results obtained through the bottom-up method, a technique often referred to as triangulation"; top-down is preferred when granular data is missing or too costly — [PrepLounge forum](https://www.preplounge.com/consulting-forum/top-down-vs-bottom-up-estimations-17115)
- **Divergence is a reality check, not a failure.** Stanford Biodesign worked example: "When top-down and bottom-up market estimates vary substantially, it's not a cause for concern! More than anything, it's a healthy 'reality check'"; the bottom-up figure often reflects today's solution space while top-down captures the total opportunity for a better solution — [Stanford Biodesign, Top-Down and Bottom-Up Market Sizing Example (PDF)](https://biodesignguide.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Top-Down-and-Bottom-Up-Market-Sizing-Example.pdf)
- **Stated tolerances (all practitioner heuristics, none authoritative).** "If two structures land close, the number is more trustworthy"; worked coffee example lands top-down vs bottom-up "within about 15%"; "within about 25% of reality is excellent" — [StrategyCase](https://strategycase.com/?p=37611). A market-sizing skill file says cross-validate and reconcile within 20%, and revisit assumptions if the gap is larger — [agentskillsfinder market-sizing skill](https://agentskillsfinder.com/skills/market-sizing). A deck guide says a modest gap "such as 15%" builds confidence — [Deckary](https://deckary.com/blog/market-sizing-template). Another skill page computes a "tam_divergence" fraction and iteratively reconciles until top-down and bottom-up triangulate — [claudepluginhub market-research skill](https://www.claudepluginhub.com/skills/therealtimex-research-ops-skills-research-ops/market-research)
- **"At least two methods" as the credibility norm.** "The most credible TAM analyses use at least two different methodologies to validate findings. When different approaches yield similar results, confidence in your market sizing increases substantially"; document every assumption with data sources and dates — [Monetizely](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/total-addressable-market-tam-the-north-star-metric-for-strategic-growth)
- **Why top-down and bottom-up diverge.** Top-down "can sometimes overstate the opportunity if not carefully segmented"; bottom-up works best "where contract values and customer counts are well-defined" — [InvestGlass](https://www.investglass.com/?p=50737). Third-party analyst estimates "are often dated, broadly defined, and disconnected from the account-level reality of who can actually buy your product" — [HG Insights](https://hginsights.com/blog/total-addressable-market-explained-definition-examples-and-calculations/). "Despite all the available data sources, the specific number you are looking for does not exist in any of the reports" — [Product Marketing Alliance](https://www.productmarketingalliance.com/common-challenges-of-tam-research/)
- **Bottom-up as the route that survives scrutiny.** "Bottom-up counts customers and multiplies by price, and it is the method that survives scrutiny" — [B. D. Emerson](https://www.bdemerson.com/article/total-addressable-market)
- **A sell-side example of triangulating against third parties.** New Constructs (Oct 2020) preferred "third-party sources to get an unbiased look at the market opportunity" and combined two third-party forecasts (data-warehouse-as-a-service $10.9B by 2027, Prescient & Strategic Intelligence; BI & analytics $55.2B by 2027, Coherent Market Insights) into a $66.1B 2027 TAM, vs. Snowflake's own $81B — [New Constructs](https://www.newconstructs.com/?p=64519)

### Inferences
- A defensible rule for a method doc, consistent with all the above: two independent routes should agree within roughly ±25–30% (a factor of ~1.3) for a mature, well-defined market; a gap up to a factor of 2–3 is common for new categories and must be explained by a named definitional difference (e.g., today's served spend vs. total opportunity, TAM vs. SAM, units vs. revenue); beyond a factor of ~3, one route is probably sizing a different thing. The "factor of two or three" figure is an inference — no source states it.
- The reconciliation move is always the same: find the single assumption whose change closes most of the gap, then decide which route's version of that assumption is better evidenced.

### Gaps
- No authoritative source (McKinsey, CFA Institute, academic) states a numeric acceptable divergence; all tolerances found are coaching or tooling heuristics.
- No source documents how a broker research department institutionally reconciles top-down data vendor numbers with company-reported figures.

## KQ4. Equity research practice: how sell-side analysts size TAM for IPOs/new lines, what data they use, and the public critiques of S-1 TAM claims (Uber, WeWork, Peloton, Snowflake)

### Takeaway
S-1 TAMs are company-authored, built by broad "every conceivable customer" definitions (Uber: all passenger-vehicle and transit miles on earth = $5.7T; WeWork: all office spend for 255M desk workers = $3T; Snowflake: $81B vs third-party $66B), and the critiques converge on a method standard: constrain the TAM to customers reachable with today's product, show the path from TAM to the company's own share, and check against independent third-party sizes. The most durable critique framework is Damodaran's "3P test" (possible / plausible / probable ↔ TAM overreach / expanded TAM / constrained TAM) and the Cornell–Damodaran "Big Market Delusion" paper (FAJ 2020).

### Cited Findings
- **Uber S-1 (April 2019) TAM construction, verbatim.** "Our Personal Mobility TAM consists of 11.9 trillion miles per year, representing an estimated $5.7 trillion market opportunity in 175 countries", comprising 7.5T passenger-vehicle miles ($4.7T) and 4.4T public-transport miles ($1.0T), including "all passenger vehicle miles and all public transportation miles in all countries globally" including countries Uber had not entered. SAM: "3.9 trillion miles per year, representing an estimated $2.5 trillion market opportunity in 57 countries", limited to trips under 30 miles and excluding public transport "given the price differential between the two modes of transportation." Method: miles estimated "by multiplying the number of passenger cars in each country by our country-level estimates of miles traveled per car"; public-transport miles allocated to regions "based on their share of the population." No third-party source is named for the mobility figures (Euromonitor, American Trucking Associations and Armstrong & Associates are cited only for delivery and freight) — [Uber Form S-1, SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1543151/000119312519103850/d647752ds1.htm)
- **Contemporaneous critique of Uber's number.** "These TAM estimates are always sky-high. The idea here is to work your way down to something more relevant, while using the TAM and SAM to justify higher valuations... this estimation seems like a pretty big stretch" — [Chicago Booth ChiBus, Apr 2019](https://www.chibus.com/perspectives/2019/4/23/uber-will-not-be-worth-120b-when-it-goes-public)
- **The Gurley–Damodaran Uber debate (2014) as the canonical TAM-definition dispute.** Damodaran valued Uber at ~$5.9B assuming a $100B global taxi/car-service market and max 10% share. Bill Gurley (Benchmark, Uber board member, acknowledging bias) argued "the past can be a poor guide for the future if the future offering is materially different than the past" and "this cannot be yesterday's market": a materially better product expands the market via price elasticity and new use cases (suburban, rental-car replacement at $27B US, kids/elderly, transit supplement); evidence: SF revenue was "a healthy multiple" of the historical taxi/limo market. His alternative: car-for-hire at 3–6× historical, plus 2.5–12.5% capture of a ~$6T car-ownership cost pool (~1B cars × ~$6,000/yr) = $150–750B; combined TAM ~$450B–$1.3T; required share for 25× Damodaran's number: ~56% (bear) to ~20% (his likely case). He also warns "seemingly innocuous assumptions can have a major impact on the output" — [Gurley, Above the Crowd, 11 Jul 2014](https://abovethecrowd.com/2014/07/11/how-to-miss-by-a-mile-an-alternative-look-at-ubers-potential-market-size/)
- **Damodaran's 3P test for TAM (Sept 2019, "Insights on VC pricing: lessons from Uber, WeWork and Peloton").** Three tiers of claim: "IT IS POSSIBLE: This could happen, but you are not sure what 'this' is, when it will happen and what it will look like" ↔ **TAM Overreach**: "Count every conceivable customer, now or in the future, as part of potential market" (assessability: "cannot assess"); "IT IS PLAUSIBLE: ... a reasoned argument could happen, though you have little tangible evidence" ↔ **Expanded TAM**: "Start with existing market reach, but expand to include new customers drawn in both product/service changes and market trends" ("Low"); "IT IS PROBABLE: ... some basis or evidence for that expectation" ↔ **Constrained TAM**: "Given product/service characteristics, count only customers that you can reach today" ("Increasing"). He observes: "almost every company that went public this year framed its total market as implausibly big" and VC pricing rewards "unrealistic (but huge) total addressable markets over plausible ones"; Lyft called itself a "transportation company", Uber a "personal mobility company", WeWork a "community company", and Peloton "a technology, media, software, product, experience, fitness, design, retail, apparel and logistics company" — [Damodaran, NYU Stern slide deck (PDF)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/blog/PelotonIPO.pdf); blog version: [Damodaran Substack, 17 Sep 2019](https://aswathdamodaran.substack.com/p/insights-on-vc-pricing-lessons-from-19-09-17)
- **Damodaran's sanity check on Peloton's growth story.** His base case pushed Peloton revenue to "just above $10 billion" by year 10, which he calls "a lofty goal, given that the largest US fitness companies (gyms and equipment makers) have revenues of $2-$3 billion" — an explicit analog-based plausibility test on the implied end-state — [Damodaran deck](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/blog/PelotonIPO.pdf)
- **"The Big Market Delusion" (Cornell & Damodaran, Financial Analysts Journal 76(2):15–25, 2020).** Big markets draw in overconfident entrepreneurs and VCs, producing collective overpricing and "an inevitable correction"; markets fill with too many firms each overestimating its odds; case studies: 1990s dot-com retail (played out), online advertising (unfolding), cannabis (beginning). Prescription: "reality checks of investment pitches for young companies via the development of skeptical discounted cash flow models based on critical evaluations of the market size and projections of the number of competitors" — [CFA Institute / FAJ](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2020/0015198x-2020-1730655); [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ufajxx/v76y2020i2p15-25.html). Damodaran's companion post (30 Dec 2019): "Uber estimated its total accessible market (TAM) to be in excess of $6 trillion"; Slack claimed "5 million plus prospective clients"; companies use "nonsensical notions of what accessible means to get to large numbers" and rarely explain why the specific company is positioned to capture the growth — [Damodaran Substack, 30 Dec 2019](https://aswathdamodaran.substack.com/p/the-market-is-huge-revisiting-the-19-12-30)
- **WeWork (Aug 2019 S-1).** Framed its market as the ~$3T businesses spend on office space for ~255M desk workers worldwide (anyone "in an occupation that requires a desk" counted as a potential member), with only 0.2% of them then in WeWork spaces; 149M potential members in current markets, 255M across 280 target markets; TechCrunch reported a $945B "potential addressable market based on 2019 membership revenue figures" for the 149M. Real-estate analysts told Business Insider the numbers "vastly overstate the company's market opportunity" because the product does not fit many business types and is unproven outside city centres — [TechCrunch, 14 Aug 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/08/14/wework-reveals-ipo-filing/); [Propmodo, Sep 2019](https://propmodo.com/despite-valuation-concerns-weworks-s-1-filing-is-a-glimpse-into-the-office-market-of-the-future/)
- **Peloton (Aug 2019 S-1).** TAM = 67M households (45M US) in current and announced markets, defined as the market reachable over the long term; within it, 52M households "interested in learning more" before seeing price; SAM = 14M Connected Fitness Products (12M US), addressable with current verticals and price points; "Historically, our SAM has grown as our brand awareness has increased." Secondary coverage frequently mislabels these (e.g., calling 67M the US TAM) — [Peloton Form S-1, SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1639825/000119312519230923/d738839ds1.htm). New Constructs called Peloton's TAM claims "outrageous" and compared them to WeWork — [New Constructs](https://www.newconstructs.com/?p=54696)
- **Snowflake (Aug/Sep 2020 S-1).** Management put the cloud data platform market at $81B — [Motley Fool, 25 Aug 2020](https://www.fool.com/investing/2020/08/25/amazon-and-microsoft-rival-snowflake-files-for-ipo/). New Constructs (Matt Shuler, 7 Oct 2020) called $81B higher than third-party figures, built a $66.1B 2027 TAM from two third-party forecasts, and argued the IPO price implied ~46% share of that 2027 TAM (~$30.3B revenue) vs <1% then, implausible against Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Oracle, Teradata and open source — [New Constructs](https://www.newconstructs.com/?p=64519). Snowflake later raised its own TAM (e.g., $248B for 2026 per a 2022 report) — [Motley Fool, Dec 2022](https://www.fool.com/investing/2022/12/15/could-snowflake-stock-help-you-become-a-millionair)
- **Data sources practitioners actually list.** "Analyst reports, Statista, Owler, LinkedIn, government reports, industry association research reports, vendor-sponsored and promoted research" — [Product Marketing Alliance](https://www.productmarketingalliance.com/common-challenges-of-tam-research/). Top-down starts from "industry reports and broad market research" then filters by geography/industry/customer profile; bottom-up gathers "number of potential customers, average revenue per user, and annual contract value" — [InvestGlass](https://www.investglass.com/?p=50737)

### Inferences
- The recurring critique pattern (Gurley vs Damodaran, New Constructs vs Snowflake, analysts vs WeWork) is a disagreement about *market definition*, not arithmetic: "today's served spend" vs "all spend the product could in principle displace". A method doc should force the author to state which tier (constrained / expanded / overreach) each number sits in — Damodaran's 3P test is the ready-made vocabulary.
- The implied-share check (what share of the claimed market must the company take for the valuation/plan to work; New Constructs' 46%, Gurley's 20–56%) is the sell-side's standard way of converting a TAM debate into a falsifiable claim; it transfers directly to product forecasts.
- Sell-side analysts have no proprietary sizing method; what distinguishes them is sourcing discipline (filings, third-party forecasts, trade data) and the skeptical DCF Cornell & Damodaran prescribe.

### Gaps
- No public sell-side research primer on TAM methodology was found (broker research is paywalled); claims about "channel checks" as a sizing input could not be sourced and are omitted.
- The WeWork 2019 S-1 text itself was not retrieved (only 2021/2022 refilings appeared), so the $3T wording is cited via TechCrunch/Propmodo. The Uber S-1's "Market, Industry, and Other Data" caveat section was not read.
- No CFA Institute or academic paper specifically auditing S-1 TAM accuracy ex post was found.

## KQ5. Diffusion and penetration: Bass model, S-curves, analog curves, and the simplest defensible version for a PM

### Takeaway
The Bass model (Bass 1969, *Management Science*) with three parameters — m (market potential), p (innovation, typically ~0.01–0.03/yr) and q (imitation, typically ~0.3–0.5/yr, mean ~0.38) — is the standard way to turn a TAM into a year-by-year adoption path; with no sales history, p and q are borrowed from analogous products. The simplest defensible PM version is: pick m from your constrained TAM, borrow (p, q) from 3–5 analogs, and show fast/base/slow scenarios, re-fitting once ≥5 periods of actuals exist.

### Cited Findings
- **Bass 1969 citation and equation.** Bass, F. M. (1969), "A new product growth for model consumer durables," *Management Science* 15(5):215–227, doi:10.1287/mnsc.15.5.215. Hazard form: f(t)/(1−F(t)) = p + qF(t), i.e. dF/dt = (1−F)(p+qF); sales s(t) = m·f(t). p = coefficient of innovation (external influence/advertising), q = coefficient of imitation (internal influence/word of mouth), m = ultimate market potential. Peak-sales time t* = (ln q − ln p)/(p+q); if p > q sales decline from launch — [Wikipedia: Bass diffusion model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_diffusion_model)
- **Typical parameter values (empirical generalisations).** Mahajan, Muller & Bass (1995), "Diffusion of new products: Empirical generalizations and managerial uses," *Marketing Science* 14(3):G79–G88, doi:10.1287/mksc.14.3.G79: p averages ~0.03 (typically 0.01–0.03), q averages ~0.38 (typically 0.3–0.5), time in years. Sultan, Farley & Lehmann (1990), "A Meta-Analysis of Applications of Diffusion Models," *JMR* 27(1):70–77, applied the model to 213 product categories — [Wikipedia: Bass diffusion model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_diffusion_model). Forecast Pro's software guidance: p between 0 and 0.1, q between 0.3 and 0.5; fit p and q if ≥5 historical data points, otherwise set them (and m) by judgement/analogy — [Forecast Pro documentation](https://forecastpro.com/Resource-Center/v14/Documentation/pages/bass-diffusion.html)
- **Intuition for p vs q.** Higher p = quicker initial adoption; higher q = slower start but faster word-of-mouth spread; q=0 reduces to exponential, p=0 to logistic — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_diffusion_model); [Forecast Pro](https://forecastpro.com/Resource-Center/v14/Documentation/pages/bass-diffusion.html)
- **Analog estimation in practice.** OLED-phone case: fit p, q, m to analogous products by non-linear regression, then take weighted averages of p and q across similar products — [Dolcera wiki, Bass analysis for OLED phones](https://dolcera.com/wiki/index.php/Bass_Diffusion_Analysis_for_OLED_display_phones). Analog forecasting = "comparing [a new offering's] adoption cycle to a functionally similar product for which sales data are available" (e.g., colour/flat-screen TV adoption for 3D TV) — [Michelfelder & Morrin 2013, Rutgers](https://crab.rutgers.edu/users/richmich/NVF/Michelfelder%20Morrin%202013%20New%20Venture%20Sales%20(5).doc). Cronrath & Zock (2007) extend a System-Dynamics Bass model with Thomas's framework of "forecasting by analogies, i.e. existing products with a high degree of similarity to an innovation" — [System Dynamics Society proceedings](https://proceedings.systemdynamics.org/2007/proceed/papers/CRONR444.pdf)
- **Known pitfalls.** Bass-based forecasts commonly show the S-curve (slow start, acceleration, taper as the pool of potential buyers shrinks); the model "has limited ability to account for changes in marketing mix after launch"; "those enthusiastic about a new product category often overestimate short-term growth and, as a result, underestimate long-term growth" — [Marketing Study Guide](https://www.marketingstudyguide.com/using-the-bass-forecasting-model/). Goodwin, Dyussekeneva & Meeran describe using the Bass model on new-product time series as "problematic" and study analogies for annual electronics sales — [Swansea Cronfa record](https://cronfa.swan.ac.uk/Record/cronfa43560). Hoisl: category adoption "initially follows an exponential curve", making timing hard to judge — [SPM Summit speaker page](https://spmsummit.org/europe/speakers-2023/2023/3/8/barbara-hoisl-independent-consultant-amp-trainer). Discrete- and continuous-time Bass forms give different p, q, and diverge outside p≈0.01–0.03, q≈0.2–0.4 (Orbach 2016) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_diffusion_model)
- **Extensions.** Generalized Bass (Bass, Krishnan & Jain 1994) adds price/advertising; Norton & Bass (1987) handles successive product generations; negative p/q variants capture adoption barriers and resistance — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_diffusion_model)

### Inferences
- Simplest defensible PM version: (1) m = constrained/serviceable TAM in units (not revenue); (2) borrow p and q from the Mahajan–Muller–Bass means (p≈0.03, q≈0.38) or from 3–5 named analogs; (3) compute cumulative F(t) for years 1–10 and the peak year t* = ln(q/p)/(p+q) ≈ 6 years at the mean values; (4) run low/base/high on q (word-of-mouth strength is the dominant uncertainty); (5) once ≥5 periods of actuals exist, re-fit. This is a synthesis of the sources above, not a quoted recipe.
- Because adoption in year 1–2 is dominated by p·m, early sales are a weak test of m; a PM should not update the TAM on the first year's actuals alone.

### Gaps
- No single practitioner guide for analog-curve selection (how to choose analogs, how to normalise curves) was found; the workflow above is inferred. Mahajan–Muller–Bass 1995 and Sultan 1990 were not read directly (paywalled); parameter values are cited via Wikipedia.

## KQ6. The 3–6 most durable public references to cite

### Takeaway
Six references cover the craft end-to-end and have stable, citable homes: Bass (1969) and Mahajan–Muller–Bass (1995) for diffusion; Cornell & Damodaran (2020) and Damodaran's 2019 "3P test" deck for TAM standards and critique; Gurley (2014) for the market-expansion counter-argument; MacGregor & Armstrong (1994) for the evidence on decomposition; plus the open-access Mahajan *Street-Fighting Mathematics* and Weinstein & Adam *Guesstimation* for estimation technique, and StrategyCase for the explicit consulting routine.
