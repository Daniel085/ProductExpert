# Public Data Sources and Uncertainty-Handling Techniques for Auditable Bottom-Up Market Sizing

Research date: 2026-10-09. Scope: (a) authoritative free sources for counting customers, firms, workers, households and devices, and for finding prices and spend; (b) techniques and tools for carrying uncertainty through a sizing; (c) how to document a sizing so every factor carries a source and a range. Sources are marked [official], [vendor], [secondary] or [paywalled] where relevant. Fetch failures (403/503) are flagged in Gaps so the writer knows which claims rest on search snippets rather than the primary page.

## Key Question 1: Counting people, firms, workers, households and devices (what each source gives, at what grain, with what caveats)

### Takeaway
For the US, the Census Bureau's CBP (establishments), SUSB (firms, by enterprise size), Nonemployer Statistics (self-employed), Economic Census (receipts/product lines, every 5 years) and ACS (people/households), plus BLS OEWS (workers by occupation), form a complete, free, NAICS/SOC-coded counting stack; the EU/UK/Canada/OECD equivalents are Eurostat SBS, ONS IDBR counts, StatCan Canadian Business Counts and OECD SDBS. Each has a known exclusion list, a 1-2.5-year lag and disclosure noise; commercial firmographic and app/web-traffic databases fill gaps but are self-reported or modelled and have no published, independently audited error rates.

### Cited Findings

**US Census Bureau: County Business Patterns (CBP)** [official]
- Data items: "Number of establishments, employment during the week of March 12, first-quarter payroll, and annual payroll"; covers "establishments with paid employees." Geography: U.S., state, county, Metropolitan/Micropolitan Statistical Area, Combined Statistical Area, ZIP code and Congressional District; Puerto Rico and Island Areas at state and county-equivalent levels. Tabulated by employment size of establishment; legal form of organization for U.S. and states only. Published annually since 1964. — [Census Bureau, CBP About](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/cbp/about.html)
- Exclusions: Crop and Animal Production, Rail Transportation, Postal Service, several insurance/pension/trust categories, Offices of Notaries, Private Households, Public Administration, and "most establishments reporting government employees"; self-employed not covered (see Nonemployer Statistics). — [Census Bureau, CBP About](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/cbp/about.html)
- Disclosure protection: "Noise infusion methodology is applied to protect individual business establishments from disclosure" (since 2007; complementary cell suppression before that). The page carries a notice that its information "is no longer current" because the Bureau is evaluating alternatives to comply with a Commerce Department order prohibiting noise infusion — i.e. the disclosure method is in flux as of 2026. — [Census Bureau, CBP About](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/cbp/about.html)

**US Census Bureau: Statistics of U.S. Businesses (SUSB)** [official]
- "An annual series that provides subnational economic data by establishment industry & enterprise size." Items: number of firms, number of establishments, employment (week of March 12), annual payroll; receipts only for years ending in 2 and 7. Geography: U.S., state, territory, county, Metro/Micro area, Congressional District. Size classes are by employment size of enterprise, developed with the SBA Office of Advocacy. — [Census Bureau, SUSB About](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/susb/about.html)
- Lag: data available "about 2 to 2½ years after each reference year." Dynamic data (births, deaths, expansions, contractions) discontinued after 2015-2016; the page points to Business Dynamics Statistics for such time series. Same industry exclusions as CBP; "SUSB also excludes most establishments reporting government employees." — [Census Bureau, SUSB About](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/susb/about.html)
- Grain distinction (inference from the two pages): CBP counts establishments (physical locations) and SUSB counts firms (enterprises) with enterprise-level size bands — the firm count is the right denominator for "number of companies that could buy," the establishment count for "number of sites."

**US Census Bureau: Nonemployer Statistics (NES)** [official]
- Counts businesses "with no paid employees or payroll that are subject to federal income tax and have receipts of $1,000 or more ($1 or more for Construction)"; most are self-employed sole proprietorships. Source is IRS business income tax records; noise infusion applied. Geography: U.S., states, counties, Metro/Micro areas, CSAs. Excludes Crop Production, Animal Production and Aquaculture, Postal Service, Funds and Trusts, Management of Companies, Private Households, Public Administration. Combined CBP+NES report ran for reference years 2012-2021 and was discontinued in 2022; from reference year 2023 the Annual Integrated Economic Survey Nonemployer Statistics (AIES-NES) provides national-level employer+nonemployer data. — [Census Bureau, NES About](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/nonemployer-statistics/about.html)

**US Census Bureau: Economic Census** [official]
- Taken "Every five years, for years ending in 2 and 7." Core questions cover operational status, primary business activity, sales or revenue, employment and payroll, and product-level sales. 2022 products cover over 950 detailed NAICS industries across 19 sectors; Geographic Area Statistics cover nearly 21,000 areas. About 4.2 million of 8+ million represented locations receive questionnaires; "For the remaining small businesses, administrative records are used in lieu of direct reporting." All 2022 releases "were complete by March 2026" — i.e. roughly a 3-4 year lag for the full set. In-scope establishments must have paid employees and be in one of 19 in-scope sectors. — [Census Bureau, Economic Census About](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/economic-census/about.html)

**US Census Bureau: American Community Survey (ACS)** [official]
- 1-year estimates: only for "areas with populations of 65,000+"; most current but smallest sample; use when "Currency is more important than precision." 5-year estimates: all areas including tracts; largest sample, most reliable, least current (pools 60 months). 1-year released annually since 2005; 5-year since 2009. — [Census Bureau, ACS Estimates guidance](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs/guidance/estimates.html)
- Topics include jobs and occupations, educational attainment, veterans, housing tenure; data "helps guide how trillions of federal dollars are distributed each year." — [Census Bureau, ACS About](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs/about.html)

**BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS)** [official]
- About 830 occupations under the Standard Occupational Classification (May 2025 estimates use 2018 SOC and 2022 NAICS). Geography: national, states/DC/territories, and ~530 metropolitan and nonmetropolitan areas. Industry: national estimates by NAICS sector, 3-digit, most 4-digit, selected 5- and 6-digit. Sample ~1.1 million establishments over 3 years, two semiannual panels of ~186-189k each; each establishment surveyed at most once every three years. Annual release, May reference date. Covers wage and salary workers in nonfarm establishments; excludes self-employed, owners/partners in unincorporated firms, household workers, unpaid family workers. May 2025 uses a new model-based method (MB3) drawing on three years of data, which affects year-to-year comparability. — [BLS, OEWS at a glance](https://www.bls.gov/oes/oes_emp.htm)

**NAICS** [official]
- NAICS is a "six-digit hierarchical coding system" classifying economic activity into twenty sectors (five goods-producing, fifteen services-providing); introduced in 1997 to replace the four-digit SIC; "periodically revised"; developed with Canada and Mexico; creates time-series breaks versus SIC. BLS links to the 2022 NAICS implementation schedule. — [BLS, NAICS overview](https://www.bls.gov/bls/naics.htm)

**SBA Office of Advocacy** [official; page fetch blocked]
- The Office of Advocacy publishes "Frequently Asked Questions About Small Business, 2024" (listed July 23, 2024) and describes it as its most-requested publication, updated annually; a 2024 Advocacy headline reports a "Small Business Total Reaches 34.8 million, Accounting for 2.6 million Net New Jobs." The FAQ and PDF returned HTTP 403 to fetch, so the 34.8M figure and the employer/nonemployer split could not be verified against the document text. — [SBA Advocacy FAQ category](https://advocacy.sba.gov/category/research/frequently-asked-questions); [SBA Advocacy tag listing with headline](https://advocacy.sba.gov/tag/entrepreneurship/page/5)
- SUSB's enterprise size classes were "developed with the SBA Office of Advocacy," so SBA firm counts and SUSB firm counts share a source. — [Census Bureau, SUSB About](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/susb/about.html)

**Eurostat Structural Business Statistics (SBS)** [official]
- Variables: number of active enterprises, turnover, value added, output, gross operating surplus, employment (employees and self-employed), hours worked, wages, productivity measures. Coverage: NACE Rev.2 sections B-E (industry), F (construction), G (trade), H-N, P-R, S95, S96; sections A (agriculture) and O (public administration) excluded; "Many countries publish at NACE 4-digit or finer national levels." Size classes: micro (<10 persons employed), small (10-49), medium (50-249), large (250+); SME = 1-249 persons and turnover ≤ EUR 50m or balance sheet ≤ EUR 43m. Geography: EU, EFTA and enlargement countries; a regional breakdown (local-unit counts, employment, wages) exists but NUTS level not stated on the page. — [Eurostat, SBS information on data](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/structural-business-statistics/information-data)
- Release lag: preliminary data published at T+12 months (usually December); final validated data published at T+22 months (usually October); exceptional revisions roughly once a year. "SBS does generally not collect information on products" (use PRODCOM / external trade for products). Sources vary by country (surveys, administrative records, business registers, estimates). — [Eurostat, SBS information on data](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/structural-business-statistics/information-data)

**ONS (UK): UK Business: activity, size and location** [official]
- Counts VAT- and/or PAYE-registered businesses (enterprises) and local units from the Inter-Departmental Business Register (IDBR); snapshot 13 March 2026; latest release 24 September 2026: 2.79 million businesses (+1.9%) and 3.2 million local units (+1.6%). Industry by SIC (a new coding tool, ClassifAI, replaced Gcode in late 2025, with 93% agreement at five-digit level). Geography: UK's 12 regions and countries; warns that multiple registrations at one address can distort small-area figures. Unregistered (below VAT/PAYE threshold) businesses are excluded and are instead covered by the Department for Business's Business Population Estimates. — [ONS, UK business: activity, size and location](https://www.ons.gov.uk/businessindustryandtrade/business/activitysizeandlocation/bulletins/ukbusinessactivitysizeandlocation/latest)

**Statistics Canada: Canadian Business Counts** [official; main pages returned 500/503]
- StatCan's Business Register user guide states the products are produced every June and December and released in August and February; employment size ranges come in a standard set of 9 (custom sets of 13 or 21 by request); since December 2014, "businesses without employees" include enterprises that are incorporated or show at least $30,000 in revenue, a change that reclassified ~70,000 businesses. Search terms "Canadian Business Counts" / "Canadian Business Patterns" locate every iteration. (Reported via search snippet; the guide page itself returned 503.) — [StatCan, Business Register Data Products User Guide](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/en/statistical-programs/document/1105_D16_V4)
- Example table: 33-10-0763, "Canadian Business Counts, with employees, census metropolitan areas and census subdivisions, June 2024" — location counts by employment size range and NAICS. — [Open Canada record](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/6ff30f27-7827-491f-9f3e-e385d54a22ab)

**OECD Structural and Demographic Business Statistics (SDBS)** [official; OECD page returned 403]
- Dataset DSD_SDBSBSC_ISIC4@DF_SDBS_ISIC4 ("Structural business statistics by size class and economic activity, ISIC Rev. 4") reports value added, operating surplus, employment and number of business units to the 4-digit ISIC Rev.4 class, by enterprise size class (persons employed); time span 1995-2024; provider last updated June 5, 2025; covers OECD members, partners and European Statistical System members. A separate business-demography series covers births, deaths, survival, high-growth enterprises; OECD notes that "employer" indicators (≥1 employee) are more comparable internationally than all-enterprise indicators, which are sensitive to business-register coverage. — [DB.nomics mirror of OECD SDBS](https://db.nomics.world/OECD/DSD_SDBSBSC_ISIC4@DF_SDBS_ISIC4); [AllThatStats catalogue of SDBS business demography](https://www.allthatstats.com/en/statistics/oecd_sdbs_bdi_isic4/sdbs-business-demography-indicators)

**World Bank World Development Indicators (WDI)** [official]
- "contains 1,400 time series indicators" for "217 economies and more than 40 country groups," many "back more than 50 years"; bulk files "are revised whenever the WDI is updated" (no fixed schedule stated on page; license not stated on this page). — [World Bank, WDI](https://datatopics.worldbank.org/world-development-indicators/)

**Firmographic databases (LinkedIn Sales Navigator, ZoomInfo, Crunchbase, D&B)** [vendor / secondary]
- Sales Navigator: strength is freshness because "profiles are maintained by the people they describe"; weakness is scope, since it "only reflects what people publish on LinkedIn," with no company financials beyond what members make public. ZoomInfo's own materials claim contact and company data "verified at over 95% accuracy" (vendor claim). EU coverage post-GDPR reported weaker (single third-party review). Crunchbase is primarily private-company data with limited people-level coverage. No independent audit of firmographic accuracy across these tools was found. — [ZoomInfo comparison page (vendor)](https://pipeline.zoominfo.com/sales/linkedin-sales-navigator-vs-zoominfo); [G2 comparison](https://www.g2.com/compare/linkedin-sales-navigator-vs-zoominfo); [knowlee.ai comparison (secondary)](https://www.knowlee.ai/compare/zoominfo-vs-linkedin-sales-navigator)

**App-store and web-traffic estimators (Sensor Tower / data.ai, Similarweb)** [vendor]
- Sensor Tower announced its acquisition of data.ai (formerly App Annie) on March 18, 2024; terms undisclosed; "both companies will remain as standalone offerings for now." — [Sensor Tower press release](https://sensortower.com/press/press-release-sensor-tower-acquires-market-intelligence-platform-data-ai); [Mobile Marketing Magazine](https://mobilemarketingmagazine.com/?p=120979)
- Definitional differences matter: Sensor Tower download numbers exclude app updates, re-downloads and multi-device installs by the same user, whereas data.ai's included those and third-party Android stores — so the two providers' totals differ by definition, not only by model. Apple does not publish iOS download counts, so third-party iOS figures cannot be checked against an official total. Sensor Tower describes "proprietary modeling and panel-based methodologies" and a 2025 pipeline combining "panels and models"; no official error rate for its download counts was found. A competitor (Appfigures) reports its own median absolute percent error at 5-25%; another competitor blog says providers disagree by 20-50% on the same app's revenue. — [Statista explainer on app downloads](https://www.statista.com/statistics/271644/worldwide-free-and-paid-mobile-app-store-downloads/); [Sensor Tower solutions (vendor)](https://sensortower.com/solutions); [Sonar blog (competitor, treat with caution)](https://trysonar.app/blog/app-revenue-estimates-how-they-work-and-fail.md)
- Similarweb data sources: first-party analytics shared by site/app owners, a "contributors network" of anonymous device traffic, public data, and partner data (DSPs, ISPs, measurement firms); claims ~10 billion signals/day, 100M+ websites, 4M+ apps; estimation is by "AI algorithms" with no published error bounds on the page; links to a methodology support article. — [Similarweb, Our Data](https://www.similarweb.com/corp/ourdata/); methodology article: https://support.similarweb.com/hc/en-us/articles/360001631538-Similarweb-Data-Methodology

### Inferences
- A defensible "number of firms by size band and industry" for the US is SUSB (firms, enterprise size) cross-checked with CBP (establishments) and NES (self-employed); the three together cover employers and nonemployers, and the exclusion lists overlap enough that a sizing in agriculture, government, rail, postal, funds/trusts or private households must use a different source.
- Lags are systematic: CBP/SUSB ~2-2.5 years, Economic Census 3-4 years for the full release, Eurostat SBS 12-22 months, ACS 5-year pools 60 months. A factor sourced from these should carry the reference year in the register and a growth adjustment with its own range.
- Noise infusion (CBP, NES) and preliminary/final revision cycles (Eurostat) mean even "official" counts are not exact; the register should record them as point estimates with a small, explicit error band rather than as truths.
- Commercial firmographic counts (LinkedIn, ZoomInfo, Crunchbase) are useful for segment-specific denominators that official statistics do not cut (e.g., "companies using technology X," "firms with a VP of RevOps") but should be recorded as self-reported/modelled with a wider range, and reconciled against SUSB totals for the same NAICS/size band to detect coverage bias.
- App-store and web-traffic estimates should be recorded with the provider's definition (installs vs. new users vs. re-downloads) because definitional differences alone can shift the count materially.

### Gaps
- Census NAICS page (census.gov/naics) returned 403; the 5-year revision cadence and NAICS-NACE-ISIC concordance tables were not confirmed from a primary page (BLS page confirms 1997 origin, six-digit hierarchy, periodic revision only).
- SBA Office of Advocacy FAQ (page and PDF) returned 403; the 34.8M small-business count and employer/nonemployer split rest on a search-snippet headline only.
- StatCan Canadian Business Counts subject page returned 500 and the user guide 503; details come from search snippets of the guide and an Open Canada record.
- OECD SDBS page returned 403; dataset details come from the DB.nomics mirror.
- ACS sample size page returned 503; annual address sample size and MOE guidance not captured.
- No independent, published accuracy audit was found for LinkedIn Sales Navigator, ZoomInfo, Crunchbase, D&B, Sensor Tower or Similarweb counts; all accuracy figures located are vendor or competitor claims.
- World Bank WDI license (CC BY 4.0) and update schedule were not stated on the fetched page.

## Key Question 2: Finding spend and price (public filings, trade associations, government spend, procurement, pricing pages, review sites; backing out ACV/ARPU)

### Takeaway
SEC XBRL APIs make per-company revenue and (where disclosed) ARR and customer counts machine-readable and free; ARPU/ACV can be backed out as ARR ÷ customers only after normalising each company's own ARR and customer definitions, which filings explicitly warn are not comparable. Government procurement (USAspending/FPDS) and contract-benchmark vendors (Vendr) give transaction-level price evidence, while G2/Capterra give list prices with known data-entry noise and low coverage.

### Cited Findings

**SEC EDGAR XBRL APIs** [official]
- `companyfacts` "returns all the company concepts data for a company into a single API call"; `companyconcept` returns one company/one concept grouped by reporting unit (USD, CAD...); `frames` "aggregates one fact for each reporting entity that is last filed" for a calendar period (annual, quarterly or instantaneous). JSON updated throughout the day with a typical XBRL processing delay of "under a minute"; bulk ZIPs republished nightly ~3:00 a.m. ET; "require no authentication or API keys." — [SEC, EDGAR APIs](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces)

**What 10-Ks disclose for ARPU/ACV back-outs** [official filings]
- SailPoint (FY2026 10-K) reports total customers plus counts by ARR band (under $250K, over $250K, over $1M) and defines ARR as "the annualized value of SaaS, maintenance, term subscription, and other subscription contracts." — [SailPoint 10-K FY2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2030781/000203078126000003/sail-20260131.htm)
- Intapp (FY2022 10-K): Cloud ARR $162.9M vs $109.7M (June 30, 2022 vs 2021); 506 and 420 clients with >$100,000 ARR, of which 41 and 31 had >$1.0M. — [Intapp 10-K FY2022](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1565687/000156459022031398/inta-10k_20220630.htm)
- Appgate (FY2022 10-K): ARR $33,656K vs $31,054K; defines a customer as "a distinct organization that has entered into a distinct agreement" whose term has not ended or whose renewal is under negotiation. — [Appgate 10-K FY2022](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1353538/000162828023010179/apgt-20221231.htm)
- HashiCorp (FY2024 10-K): one organization with multiple divisions counts as one customer; consumption-based ARR is based on the last three months of spend (definition changed Q4 FY2024). — [HashiCorp 10-K FY2024](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1720671/000162828024012350/hcp-20240131.htm)
- Samsara (FY2024/FY2026 10-Ks) warns that ARR "does not have a standardized meaning and is not necessarily comparable to similarly titled measures presented by other companies." — [Samsara 10-K FY2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1642896/000162828026018167/iot-20260131.htm)

**US federal spend: USAspending.gov / FPDS** [official + GAO/CRS]
- USAspending.gov is "a government source for data on federal awards by state, congressional district (CD), county, city, and zip code"; FFATA required a public database of federal funds to the final recipient; the DATA Act (eight years later) required Treasury and OMB to standardise government-wide data. CRS (updated April 22, 2025) says compiling accurate award data "can be challenging due, in part, to continuing data quality issues that have been identified by the U.S. Government Accountability Office." — [CRS R44027, Tracking Federal Awards (2025 update)](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-04-22_R44027_a6070ba9ac3f414e157a2b1dd12d67a47e3efbfc.html)
- Contract data flows daily into FPDS and Treasury extracts FPDS procurement data "for publication to USAspending nightly"; financial assistance data submitted at least twice monthly; recipient-reported files (E and F) come from SAM.gov. — [USDA DATA Act FAQ module](https://nfc.usda.gov/fss/clientservices/fms/DATA_Act/documents/DATA_Act_FAQs/Module_1_DATA_Act_Overview.pdf)
- GAO-20-75 (Nov 2019) found submission quality had improved but agencies still needed to disclose known data limitations (96 agencies, Q4 FY2018); GAO found grant subaward records "with missing information, impossibly large subaward amounts, and likely duplicative records." — [GAO-20-75](https://gao.gov/assets/gao-20-75.pdf)

**SaaS contract benchmarks (Vendr)** [vendor; partially paywalled]
- Vendr's Price Estimate tool draws on "data from over 30,000 contracts"; its AWS listing cites "over $3 billion in SaaS spend." 2023 reports: average ACV "dropped by 45% between Q1 and Q2 2023"; ACV hit a three-year low of $62k in Q2 2023 then $89k the next quarter; full-year 2023 blended ACV $87k (-4% YoY), with net-new ACV +32% YoY and renewal ACV -11%. Vendr defines discount as "the delta between the price offered at the beginning and end of the negotiation" and contrasts its transaction data with G2/Morgan Stanley surveys. — [Vendr SaaS Trends Report 2023](https://www.vendr.com/insights/saas-trends-report-2023); [Vendr Q2 2023](https://vendr.com/insights/saas-trends-report-q2-2023); [Vendr price-estimate docs](https://developers.vendr.com/docs/solution/price-estimate); [Tom Tunguz on Vendr Q3 report](https://tomtunguz.com/vendr-q3-report/)

**Review-site pricing (G2, Capterra)** [vendor]
- G2 (2025) reports that "only 4% of G2 product profiles explicitly list prices as of March 2025," and that G2 now shows prices on product pages; earlier G2 research found pricing information to be the number one feature B2B buyers find most helpful. — [G2 Learn, software pricing transparency](https://learn.g2.com/software-pricing-transparency)
- OpenView (2018) argued that "third party review sites like G2 Crowd, Capterra, Quora and Siftery increasingly put pricing information into the public domain." — [OpenView pricing transparency study](https://labs.openviewpartners.com/pricing-transparency-study/)
- Example of data-entry noise: a Capterra listing shows a starting price of "$18000.00" per user per month for an alternative product — almost certainly an error; do not use review-site prices unchecked. — [Capterra G2 Track page](https://capterra.com/p/239087/G2-Track)

**VC heuristics on price × quantity** [secondary, practitioner]
- Lightspeed partner Sebastian Duesterhoeft (interview published April 5, 2023): core TAM is price × quantity; where quantity is hard to pin down, "estimate typical SMB, mid-market, and enterprise spend, then multiply by the number of companies in each bucket"; per-seat products are easier to size. Benchmarks offered as cross-checks: security ≈ 7-10% of infrastructure cost; cloud security ≈ 3-5% of cloud spend; for every $100 spent on Snowflake, ~$20-25 on the rest of the modern data stack. — [Lightspeed, TAM Masterclass](https://lsvp.com/stories/a-total-addressable-market-tam-masterclass/)

### Inferences
- The clean ARPU recipe from filings is period-end ARR ÷ period-end customers, with four normalisations recorded in the register: (1) what ARR includes (SailPoint includes maintenance/term licences; Samsara excludes not-yet-live subscriptions; HashiCorp uses trailing-3-month consumption), (2) what a "customer" is (Appgate counts open or in-renewal agreements; HashiCorp rolls subsidiaries into one), (3) where only ARR bands are disclosed, bracket tier ACV rather than compute a mean, (4) prefer ARR over GAAP revenue because recognition timing distorts revenue.
- For price factors the hierarchy of evidence is: transaction data (Vendr, procurement records) > public pricing pages > review-site listings; each tier should carry a wider range in the register, and review-site prices should be verified against the vendor page before use.
- USAspending is a reasonable source for "what government buyers pay" at the award level (by NAICS/PSC/agency), but subaward and some award records have known duplication and magnitude errors, so an aggregate derived from it should be cross-checked against FPDS and the latest GAO review.

### Gaps
- USAspending.gov's own About page returned only the site title; grain (NAICS, PSC, place of performance) and update cadence come from the CRS/USDA secondary descriptions.
- No source was found that documents trade-association spend surveys' methods generically; the writer should treat trade-association figures as member-survey based unless the association states otherwise.
- Vendr's ACV series located ends in 2023; no 2024-2026 public benchmarks were found.
- No 10-K located that reports "average ARR per customer" directly; the back-out method above is a synthesis, not a quoted practitioner procedure.

## Key Question 3: Analyst reports (Gartner, IDC, Forrester, Grand View Research, Statista) — how produced, why TAMs are often unreliable, when legitimately useful

### Takeaway
Documented critiques show two failure modes: tier-one trackers (IDC/Gartner) have produced shipment estimates contradicted by vendor-reported actuals and, per a former employee, preserved growth rates over accuracy; high-volume publishers (Grand View, MarketsandMarkets, Technavio, Mordor and peers) sell undisclosed-method reports whose CAGRs can be shown wrong against later estimates and that are widely repeated by press. VCs treat analyst TAMs pasted into decks as a red flag; their legitimate use is as a top-down cross-check against a bottom-up price × quantity build.

### Cited Findings
- Engadget (Yoni Heisler, July 31, 2014), citing a former-IDC-employee account published by Fortune's Philip Elmer-DeWitt: IDC "prioritized keeping growth rates consistent over accuracy, with the 'others' category serving as a balancing plug"; data "trustworthy only for major vendors"; vendors "kept their own internal numbers and cited IDC only for public purposes." In the June 2014 quarter Apple reported "strong double digit growth" in US Mac sales while IDC and Gartner had estimated a year-over-year decline. — [Engadget, Why IDC sales estimates are complete BS](https://www.engadget.com/2014-07-31-why-idc-sales-estimates-are-complete-bs.html)
- IPVM (John Honovich, "Beware Scam Market 'Research'", first published 2017, republished March 30, 2018) documents: a Technavio security-camera press release containing a sentence about "revenue generated from the retail sales of haircare products"; a Technavio homeland-security camera report omitting Axis, Avigilon, Dahua and Hikvision; reports listing players (3M Cogent, Safran, VideoIQ) that had already exited; firms (Grand View Research, Stratistics MRC) claiming US/UK presence while LinkedIn shows nearly all staff in India; Technavio claiming coverage of 100+ industries with ~300 analysts; report prices of $1,500-$2,500+; a reader tracing a $47.5B home-security figure from MarketsandMarkets through Security Sales to a 2018 New York Times article; these firms are "the most common source cited for market projections" with numbers "groundless and often wildly wrong." — [IPVM, Beware Scam Market Research](https://ipvm.com/reports/5314)
- Searchfunder essay (author identified as Tuck School of Business affiliate; ~6 months old as of fetch): "a cohort of high-volume publishers - Mordor Intelligence, Grand View Research, MarketsandMarkets, Technavio - produce forecasts at industrial scale. These reports are not peer-reviewed, their methodologies are typically not disclosed, and their accuracy track records are not published"; reports "$4k-$5k a pop"; CAGRs "differ between executive summary and body text." Worked example: MarketsandMarkets' Dec 2018 blockchain forecast of $23.3B by 2023 (80.2% CAGR) versus three other firms' 2023 estimates of $7.5B, $12.4B and $17.46B. Names IBISWorld, Wood Mackenzie and Definitive Healthcare as using "proprietary primary data, named macro drivers, and regression analysis" and stating uncertainty; and government sources (EIA, BLS, CBO, IMF, World Bank) as more trustworthy "provided their projections are not treated as predictions." — [Searchfunder, CAGR: the cynical joke](https://searchfunder.com/post/cagr-the-cynical-joke-market-research-firms-play-at-the-expense-of-gullible-executives)
- Statista forecast construction example: for a US market chart, "data for years 2023 to 2030 has been calculated by Statista using a CAGR published by the source" — i.e. Statista layers on the upstream publisher's growth rate rather than independently estimating. — [Statista/ecommerceDB chart note](https://ecommercedb.com/insights/chart/11285)
- Lightspeed's Duesterhoeft: using Gartner or IDC TAM figures directly in a pitch is "a big red flag"; founders "often don't know what those numbers include or exclude, or how the firms estimated them"; reliance suggests the founder has not defined the customer (Q) or price (P). His own top-down cross-checks are ratio benchmarks (e.g., security as % of infrastructure spend applied to ~$150B CY22 hyperscaler revenue) and the fund-return test (a $500M fund at 20% ownership needs a $2.5B exit, ~$250M revenue at 10x; horizontal SaaS leaders reach ~20% share so that implies ~$2.5B TAM). — [Lightspeed, TAM Masterclass](https://lsvp.com/stories/a-total-addressable-market-tam-masterclass/)
- The Hustle (Oct 2020): big TAMs are used "to justify lofty valuations" and professional investors "have an adverse reaction to the phrase." — [The Hustle, TAM](https://thehustle.co/10082020-tam)
- Jon Peddie: worked example contrasting a $2B stated market with a serviceable obtainable market of about $20M. — [Jon Peddie, The fallacy of TAM](https://www.jonpeddie.com/blog/the-fallacy-of-tam/)

### Inferences
- The structural reason analyst TAMs mis-serve a bottom-up sizing: their market definition (what is in/out), unit (shipments vs. revenue vs. spend), and method (vendor surveys, "others" plug, CAGR extrapolation) are rarely disclosed, so an analyst figure cannot be decomposed into price × quantity factors that carry sources and ranges.
- Legitimate uses, consistent with the sources: (1) as a top-down cross-check — if the bottom-up build lands within the analyst range, note agreement; if outside, document which factor explains the gap; (2) for relative structure (vendor share order, segment mix) where a tracker's panel is strong (major vendors); (3) as a base rate for growth, with a wide range, never as the point estimate.
- A method doc can operationalise this by requiring any analyst number to be recorded with: publisher tier (tracker vs. high-volume publisher), stated definition, year, price paid/paywall, and the bottom-up factor it is being compared against.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or systematic accuracy study comparing analyst market-size forecasts to realised outcomes was found; critiques are journalistic/anecdotal.
- The claim circulating that a 2024 Bain analysis of 80 PE-backed software deals found the median deal achieved only 22% of its TAM-implied revenue trajectory could not be located in any Bain publication; treat as unverified.
- Gartner/IDC/Forrester methodology pages were not fetched; no primary description of their estimation process was captured.

## Key Question 4: Uncertainty techniques — calibrated 90% CIs, rule of five, Monte Carlo tools, sensitivity/tornado, low/base/high, Superforecasting practices; references for non-statisticians

### Takeaway
The accessible canon is Hubbard's *How to Measure Anything* (calibrated 90% CIs via the equivalent-bet test, the rule of five, expected value of information, Monte Carlo) and Tetlock & Gardner's *Superforecasting* (outside-view base rates first, Fermi decomposition, frequent small updates, pre-committing to what would change your mind); free tools that implement range inputs with Monte Carlo are Guesstimate (open source, "5 to 9" syntax), Squiggle (QURI), and the Causal approach (now folded into Lucanet), with tornado charts as the standard one-at-a-time sensitivity display.

### Cited Findings

**Calibrated confidence intervals (Hubbard)**
- A 90% CI is "a range of values that is 90% likely to contain the correct value"; in studies, true values fell inside subjects' 90% CIs only about half the time; "assessing uncertainty is a general skill that can be taught with a measurable improvement"; even pretending to bet money improves calibration. — [LessWrong summary of How to Measure Anything (lukeprog, 7 Aug 2013)](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ybYBCK9D7MZCcdArB/how-to-measure-anything)
- Peer-reviewed baseline: "If people were well calibrated, 90% of their 90% confidence intervals would contain the true value. However, true values typically fall within such intervals between 30% and 60% of the time" (McKenzie et al., 2008); Russo & Schoemaker (1992) found professionals' 90-95% intervals had 40-60% hit rates in their own domains. — [McKenzie et al. 2008, OBHDP (UCSD PDF)](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~cmckenzie/McKenzieetal2008OBHDP.pdf)
- Equivalent-bet test (Hubbard): compare your 90% range against a device that wins 90% of the time (ten-pocket wheel, nine black); "if you would rather spin the wheel than rely on your range, your range is too narrow"; adjust the CI until the two bets seem equally good. Other methods: repetition with feedback; testing each bound separately ("is there a 95% chance that the answer is above my lower bound?"); listing two pros and two cons for the range. — [LessWrong, How to calibrate your confidence intervals](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5XBv2onWGRKFtAEMN/link-how-to-calibrate-your-confidence-intervals); [The Uncertainty Project, Estimator calibration](https://www.theuncertaintyproject.org/tools/estimator-calibration)
- Hubbard Decision Research sells a self-paced calibration course (3-4 hours, $300) whose stated goal is that participants "are right 80% of the time they are 80% confident"; no quantitative outcome data on the page. [vendor] — [HDR, Calibrated Probability Assessment](https://hubbardresearch.com/calibrated-probability-assessment/)
- Overview of the technique with the Lichtenstein/Fischhoff finding that answers assigned 90% confidence are right only ~75% of the time. — [Wikipedia, Calibrated probability assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calibrated_probability_assessment)

**Rule of five (Hubbard)**
- "There is a 93.75% chance that the median of a population is between the smallest and largest values" in a random sample of five; it concerns the median, so it tolerates heavy-tailed distributions. Derivation: 1 − 2(0.5^5) = 0.9375. — [LessWrong summary](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ybYBCK9D7MZCcdArB/how-to-measure-anything); [DADM FAQ, Hubbard's Rule of Five (with worked math)](https://rww.quarto.pub/dadm-faq/posts/rule-of-five/index.html); [TechTarget definition](https://www.techtarget.com/whatis/definition/rule-of-five-statistics)

**Expected value of information and measurement inversion (Hubbard)**
- EVI = expected opportunity loss before minus after a measurement; "the economic value of measuring a variable is usually inversely proportional to how much measurement attention it usually gets" (measurement inversion). — [LessWrong summary](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ybYBCK9D7MZCcdArB/how-to-measure-anything)

**Monte Carlo tools**
- Guesstimate (Ozzie Gooen; public beta late 2015): cells accept ranges/distributions; "Ranges are denoted as [5th percentile, 95th percentile]"; outputs "based on 5,000 Monte Carlo simulations"; outputs mean, median and percentiles; runs in browser; motivation was that prior options were "expensive Excel plugins and statistical programming packages." — [EA Forum, Guesstimate announcement](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/Bt4nkCGHKBkDk97mn/guesstimate-an-app-for-making-decisions-with-confidence); [FlowingData write-up (Jan 2016)](https://flowingdata.com/2016/01/04/an-uncertain-spreadsheet-for-estimates)
- Guesstimate docs: "If you think a number is between 5 and 9, simply write '5 to 9'"; "uses Monte Carlo sampling to correctly estimate uncertain results"; "Create unlimited public models for free"; "Our code base is open source." — [Guesstimate docs](https://docs.getguesstimate.com/)
- Squiggle (QURI, founded 2019 by Gooen): "a minimalist programming language for probabilistic estimation" for situations where "most variables must be intuitively estimated by domain experts"; distributions are first-class values (e.g., `normal(10, 2) * uniform(0.8, 1.2)`); syntax forked from Guesstimate/Foretold; docs include a piano-tuner Fermi example. — [LongtermWiki, Squiggle](https://www.longtermwiki.com/knowledge-base/responses/epistemic-tools/tools/squiggle); [QURI](https://www.longtermwiki.com/wiki/quri)
- Causal (blog, "The Flaw of Averages"): models built on averages hide the uncertainty that should shape decisions; "Unlike a spreadsheet, your assumptions can take on uncertain values"; worked marketing example enters CPA as "$100 to $200" instead of $130, turning a $100k budget into 1,000-2,000 customers; default output is a 90% probability range; engine runs a fixed number of simulated futures (1,000 or 5,000 cited on different pages); a Google Sheets add-on ("Causal Scenarios") brought ranges into spreadsheets. Note: causal.app URLs now redirect to Lucanet (Causal was absorbed), so these posts may be unreachable. — [Causal, Flaw of Averages](https://causal.app/blog/forecasting-with-uncertainty); [Causal, Marketing vs Finance](https://www.causal.app/blog/setting-realistic-expectations-between-marketing-and-finance); [Causal Sheets add-on](https://www.causal.app/blog/google-sheets-add-on)
- Excel add-ins: Oracle Crystal Ball (paid, "Free version unavailable"), Palisade @RISK (paid), ModelRisk, RiskAMP (paid); Yasai is an open-source Excel Monte Carlo add-in; SimulAr listed as free. [secondary directory listings; verify status] — [AlternativeTo, @RISK alternatives](https://alternativeto.net/software/-risk/); [AlternativeTo, Crystal Ball alternatives](https://alternativeto.net/software/oracle-crystal-ball/)
- Monte Carlo as described for non-statisticians: "uses a computer to randomly generate thousands of possible values for each variable" from estimated ranges; a machine-lease example showed ~14% of scenarios producing a loss. — [LessWrong summary](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ybYBCK9D7MZCcdArB/how-to-measure-anything)
- Rule of thumb from a business-case article: at least 1,000 runs, more when uncertainty is high; "a team's disagreement shows up in the width of the ranges the team settles on." — [MoreThanDigital, Integrating uncertainty into business cases](https://morethandigital.info/en/integrating-uncertainty-into-business-cases-for-better-investment-decisions/)

**Sensitivity / tornado analysis**
- "Each variable is adjusted between a low value and a high value while everything else is held constant at a baseline, often called the 'base case'"; ranges "may reflect confidence intervals, literature-based estimates, or plausible scenario bounds"; bars sorted by absolute swing with the base case as a vertical line, giving the tornado shape; limitation: "inability to capture parameter interactions or joint uncertainty," which is why HTA agencies also require probabilistic sensitivity analysis. — [MetricGate, Deterministic sensitivity / tornado](https://metricgate.com/docs/deterministic-sensitivity-tornado/); [GoldSim help, Tornado chart](https://help.goldsim.com/Content/GS/sensitivityanalysistornadochart.htm); [CASRAI guide](https://casrai.org/guides/tornado-diagram-sensitivity-analysis)

**Superforecasting practices (Tetlock & Gardner)**
- Ten commandments (as summarised by Farnam Street): triage; break problems down ("flush ignorance into the open"); balance inside and outside views ("How often do things of this sort happen in situations of this sort?"); update beliefs ("Belief updating is to good forecasting as brushing and flossing are to good dental hygiene"); acknowledge the strongest counterargument and "decide in advance what evidence would change your mind"; distinguish fine degrees of probability; balance prudence and decisiveness; keep a decision journal; manage the team; deliberate practice. — [Farnam Street, Ten Commandments for Superforecasters](https://fs.blog/ten-commandments-for-superforecasters/)
- Good Judgment Project evidence: "comparison classes" (reference-class/outside-view) training correlated with accuracy while "post-mortem analysis" correlated with inaccuracy; aggregation recipe was average, weight top forecasters, then extremize. — [AI Impacts, Evidence on good forecasting practices](https://aiimpacts.org/?p=1283)
- Fermi-izing: "helps the forecasters figure out what's known and what's not known, what's knowable and what's not knowable"; "remarkably good probability estimates arise from a remarkably crude series of assumptions and guesstimates"; sequence is outside view first, inside view as an adjustment. — [Boris Smus notes on Superforecasting](https://smus.com/books/superforecasters-by-phil-tetlock/); [jcx.au review](https://blog.jcx.au/posts/tetlock-and-gardners-superforecasting-the-art-and-science-of-prediction.html)

### Inferences
- A practical minimum for a sizing register, consistent with these sources: every factor carries a calibrated 90% interval (not a ±10% convention), the interval is checked with the equivalent-bet test, and the model is run as a Monte Carlo (≥1,000-5,000 draws) rather than multiplying point estimates — because multiplying best guesses ignores the "flaw of averages" and multiplying intervals' endpoints overstates the spread.
- Low/base/high cases are the deterministic cousin of this: record them as the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles of each factor's range so they are consistent with the Monte Carlo rather than arbitrary.
- The tornado chart answers "which factor should we research next" and pairs with Hubbard's EVI logic: spend research effort on the factor with the largest swing, which is usually the least-measured one (measurement inversion).
- The rule of five gives a cheap, defensible way to bound a price or usage factor: five random customer quotes or contracts bracket the median with 93.75% confidence — a legitimate "source" entry for a factor when no public data exists, provided the sample is random.
- Superforecasting's base-rate-first discipline translates to sizing as: start from a reference class (e.g., attach rates or penetration achieved by comparable products, from filings or official statistics), then adjust for the specific case, and pre-register what evidence would move the estimate.

### Gaps
- Hubbard's own figures on post-training calibration (e.g., share of trainees reaching near-perfect calibration "within half a day") are reported only secondhand (LessWrong); no peer-reviewed replication located.
- Guesstimate's distributions page returned 404 and the homepage returned only a title; the 5,000-sample count and [5th, 95th] percentile convention come from the 2015-16 announcement and community write-ups, not current docs.
- Causal's blog is now redirected to Lucanet; details are from search snippets and may be out of date.
- Current license/maintenance status of Yasai and SimulAr was not verified.

## Key Question 5: Documenting a sizing so it is auditable (assumptions register with source, date, confidence, counter-case; public templates)

### Takeaway
No widely adopted public "assumptions register" spreadsheet for market sizing was found; the closest public artefacts are Guesstimate/Squiggle models (which force a range per cell and are shareable) and prose guidance that each input carry a visible source path, a confidence tier, labelled gaps and a sensitivity rank. The register design below is a synthesis of those sources plus the Superforecasting "what would change my mind" practice.

### Cited Findings
- Principles found in practitioner guidance: keep "sourced facts, inferred estimates, and judgment calls" distinct; where exact data is missing, use "a defensible proxy, explain why it is reasonable, and note the confidence level"; "label the gap and explain how it affects confidence"; "make the source path visible for each major input"; identify the assumptions that move the estimate most and show how the result changes when they move. — [OpenAI market-sizing agent skill (mirror)](https://mcpservers.org/agent-skills/openai/market-sizing)
- A three-tier confidence convention in a third-party skill: HIGH = "multiple corroborating sources, recent data"; MEDIUM = "single authoritative source, 1-2 years old"; LOW = "extrapolated, assumptions, old data." (A convention, not a standard.) — [Orchestkit market-sizing skill](https://skillselion.com/skills/yonatangross/orchestkit/market-sizing)
- A generic market-sizing form template covers market definition and scope, TAM/SAM/SOM, pricing inputs, and "key assumptions and data sources," on the rationale that it "creates a record of the assumptions behind each estimate." — [Typeform market sizing template](https://www.typeform.com/templates/market-sizing-template)
- Superforecasting practice for the counter-case: "Acknowledge the strongest counterargument to your view, and decide in advance what evidence would change your mind"; keep a decision journal. — [Farnam Street, Ten Commandments](https://fs.blog/ten-commandments-for-superforecasters/)
- Hubbard's calibration prompt that doubles as a register field: "Identify two pros and two cons for the range that you have given." — [LessWrong, How to calibrate your confidence intervals](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5XBv2onWGRKFtAEMN/link-how-to-calibrate-your-confidence-intervals)
- Guesstimate models are public and free by default ("Create unlimited public models for free") and open source, which makes a shared, range-per-cell model a de facto auditable artefact. — [Guesstimate docs](https://docs.getguesstimate.com/)
- Official statistics supply the "date" and "grain" fields for free: e.g., SUSB lag 2-2.5 years, Eurostat SBS preliminary at T+12 / final at T+22 months, ONS snapshot date 13 March 2026, StatCan June/December reference dates. — [Census SUSB](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/susb/about.html); [Eurostat SBS](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/structural-business-statistics/information-data); [ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/businessindustryandtrade/business/activitysizeandlocation/bulletins/ukbusinessactivitysizeandlocation/latest); [StatCan user guide](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/en/statistical-programs/document/1105_D16_V4)
- Analyst-report fields a register should force (from the critiques): publisher, whether methodology is disclosed, definition of the market, year, price/paywall. — [Searchfunder](https://searchfunder.com/post/cagr-the-cynical-joke-market-research-firms-play-at-the-expense-of-gullible-executives); [IPVM](https://ipvm.com/reports/5314)

### Inferences
- A register row per factor, synthesised from the above, would carry: factor ID and definition (unit, segment, geography); value with low/base/high as 5th/50th/95th percentiles (calibrated 90% CI); source URL, publisher and publisher tier (official statistic / filing / transaction data / vendor estimate / analyst report / judgment); reference date of the data and date accessed; grain (geography, NAICS/SOC/NACE code, size band) and any definitional note (e.g., which ARR definition, installs vs. users); confidence tier with reason (the HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW convention above); "what would make this wrong" counter-case and the evidence that would move it (Superforecasting commandment 5); two pros / two cons for the range (Hubbard); sensitivity rank from the tornado; next validation step and owner.
- Public templates worth pointing to are tool-shaped rather than spreadsheet-shaped: a Guesstimate public model (range per cell, Monte Carlo, shareable link) or a Squiggle file in version control satisfies "every factor carries a range" by construction; the source/date/counter-case columns still need a companion table.
- Free DIY Monte Carlo in plain Excel/Sheets (NORM.INV(RAND(),...) or LOGNORM.INV plus a data table) is sufficient for a 5-10 factor sizing and avoids paid add-ins; this is a widely known pattern but no authoritative page was fetched for it.

### Gaps
- No public, downloadable assumptions-register spreadsheet or notebook specifically for market sizing with source/date/confidence/counter-case columns was found; the design above is a synthesis.
- No public Guesstimate or Squiggle market-sizing model was located by search (the Squiggle docs' canonical Fermi example is piano tuners, not a market).
- Statista's and Grand View's own methodology pages were not fetched, so the register's "methodology disclosed?" test for those publishers is inferred from critiques rather than from their statements.
