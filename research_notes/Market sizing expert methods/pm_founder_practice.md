# PM / founder practice: sizing an opportunity from the customer's problem outward

Scope note: these notes cover the product-side practice of opportunity sizing — methods that start from a job-to-be-done, a segment, and what customers pay today (money and time) — not industry-report (top-down) sizing. Research date: 2026-10-09. Sources are marked **[primary]** (named author, their own outlet), **[secondary]** (book notes, course summaries) or **[vendor]** (content marketing by a pricing/data/billing vendor). Several key texts (Christensen's *Innovator's Solution*, MIT SMR 2007, *Monetizing Innovation*, *Startup Owner's Manual*) were only reachable through dated, named secondary notes; that is flagged where it matters.

---

## KQ1. Steve Blank's market types and what each implies for sizing

### Takeaway
Blank's framework is qualitative: market type (existing / resegmented / new / clone) changes the *shape* of adoption and revenue, not a sizing formula. For new markets he explicitly says TAM is effectively unknowable from the market itself, and offers two workarounds — size and project the **adjacent markets** you expect customers to come from ("petal diagram"), and define the market by the **job-to-be-done** (with Ulwick) so the market stays constant while the product changes.

### Cited Findings
- Market type set: Blank distinguishes startups entering an existing market, re-segmenting an existing market ("offering a product that costs less or addresses the specific needs of a customer segment that the existing suppliers have failed to address"), or "creating an entirely new market with a disruptive innovation that never existed before"; "Market Type also affects the market size as well as how you launch the product into the market." — [Steve Blank, Market Types category index, steveblank.com](https://steveblank.com/category/market-types/) **[primary]**
- New-market sizing via adjacent markets: "We need a different way to represent the competitive landscape when you are creating a business that never existed… what if the markets may not exist and you are creating one?" His petal diagram puts the startup at the centre and draws the adjacent segments it expects customers to come from; "you could show the current and projected market sizes of the adjacent markets which allows the startups to have a 'how big can our new market be?' conversation with investors," optionally scaling the petals "relative to market size." Example: a lifelong-learning network for entrepreneurs drew five petals (corporate, higher ed, startup ecosystem, institutions, adult learning). — [Steve Blank, "A New Way to Look at Competitors", 8 Nov 2013](https://steveblank.com/2013/11/08/a-new-way-to-look-at-competitors/) **[primary]**
- Adoption and revenue shape by type: "Existing Markets adoption rates are measured in % market share gained, New Markets have adoption rates which may occur in your company's lifetime." — [Blank, Market Types index](https://steveblank.com/category/market-types/) **[primary]**
- New-market revenue curve: an existing-market plan is "a straight linear growth line"; in a new market "the revenue projection would resemble the hockey stick"; "The small hump in year 1 is from the early adopters who buy one of anything"; "The flat part of the graph, years 1 to 4 is the Death Valley many companies never leave"; "Companies in New Markets who hire and execute like they're in an Existing Market burn through their cash." Investor benchmarks quoted in the same post: VCs favour markets over $500M and "companies that can generate $100M/year in revenue by year five." — [Steve Blank, "Death By Revenue Plan", 16 Feb 2010](https://steveblank.com/2010/02/16/death-by-revenue-plan/) **[primary]**
- Competitive analysis by type: "A competitive analysis makes sense when your startup is entering an Existing market"; in resegmented and new markets it "starts with the hypothesis of" the segment / new market rather than a feature grid. — [Steve Blank, "Death By Competitive Analysis", 1 Mar 2010](https://steveblank.com/2010/03/01/death-by-analysis/) **[primary]**
- New markets require category education and take years: for TiVo, "a company needs to educate potential customers about the space not the product… This results in a much slower adoption curve – the classic hockey stick"; "TiVo spent the next five years trying to convince users"; "Hundreds of millions of dollars went into marketing campaigns to create an entirely new consumer electronics category." — [Steve Blank, "Hubris Versus Humility: The $15 billion Difference", 8 Nov 2010](https://steveblank.com/2010/11/08/hubris-versus-humility-the-15-billion-difference/) **[primary]**
- First-mover risk in new/resegmented markets: citing Tellis & Golder, Blank notes fast followers "entered the market an average of thirteen years later than the pioneers," with a "47% failure rate" for first movers vs "8% failure rate" for fast followers; "The issue is not being first to market, but understanding the type of market your company is going to enter." — [Steve Blank, "Why Pioneers Have Arrows In Their Backs", 4 Oct 2010](https://steveblank.com/2010/10/04/why-pioneers-are-the-ones-with-the-arrows-in-their-backs/) **[primary]**
- Market definition by job (Blank with Ulwick/Thayer): "a market is best defined as: a group of people and the job they are trying to get done"; "Your market = Group of people (Step 4) + Job-to-be-Done (Step 8)"; "The market definition becomes a constant in the product/market fit equation, not a variable"; "In most cases, innovators don't create markets; they create products to serve markets." The 8-step canvas: start with the product/idea → categories of people who use it → abstract to one group label → function the product performs → other products used alongside → abstract to a core functional job → combine. Explicitly: "try not to use an actual job title"; "Do not list out influencers, economic buyers." Crypto and ride-sharing are treated as new *products* in existing job-defined markets, not new markets. The post gives no counting or sizing method. — [Steve Blank, "How to Find a Market? Use Jobs-To-Be-Done as the Front End of Customer Discovery", 4 Nov 2021](https://steveblank.com/2021/11/04/market-definition-its-the-front-end-of-customer-discovery/) **[primary]**
- Blank's course sizing vocabulary (as relayed by a university programme using his *How to Build a Startup* course): "Total Available Market: how big the market is. How many potential customers?"; "Served Available Market: how big your part of the market is. How many customers can you reach?"; "Target Market: how much of the market you can get. Who will be most likely buyers at the outset?"; "The most specified target market is found by talking with potential customers, channel partners, and competitors." Some VC firms "won't consider an investment in a company unless the market is at least $1 billion a year." — [Innovation Institute, Univ. of Pittsburgh, "How big is the pie?", 27 Jun 2016](https://blog.innovation.pitt.edu/how-big-is-the-pie) **[secondary]**
- Clone market type: secondary summaries of *The Startup Owner's Manual* list four types including "clone" (copying a proven model into a new geography) and describe the new-market type as one where "customers can't perform the task without that company" and "the learning curve is steep." — [Latitud, "TAM/SOM intro for early-stage founders"](https://latitud.com/blog/tam-som-intro-market-sizing-early-stage-founder) **[vendor/secondary]**; [UBC entrepreneurship blog](https://blogs.ubc.ca/entrepreneurship/?p=574) **[secondary]**
- Market-type effect on burn/revenue (video posts, no transcript): "Understanding 'Market Type' can save you a ton of money and time." — [Steve Blank, "Market Type and Revenue", 24 Feb 2015](https://steveblank.com/2015/02/24/market-type-and-revenue-2-minutes-to-find-out-why/) **[primary]**

### Inferences
- For Blank, sizing a new market is a two-part exercise: (a) a *ceiling* from the current and projected size of adjacent markets whose customers you expect to pull over, and (b) a *floor/timing* assumption that revenue is near zero for ~4 years, so the plan must be modelled on a hockey stick, not a share-of-existing-market line.
- Blank's 2021 JTBD canvas is the bridge to discovery evidence: because the market is "group of people + job", the count of job executors (not budget holders or job titles) is the natural unit for a bottom-up TAM, and that unit does not change when the solution does.
- The "$500M market / $100M by year five" and "$1B/yr" figures are investor screens Blank reports, not his sizing method.

### Gaps
- I could not reach the text of *The Startup Owner's Manual* or *Four Steps to the Epiphany* on market size; the "four types incl. clone" and "steep learning curve" phrasing comes from secondary summaries. The books' customer-discovery hypotheses (market size hypothesis, "day in the life", quantified customer pain) are not verified here.
- No Blank source gives a numeric method for new-market TAM beyond "adjacent markets' current and projected sizes."

## KQ2. Christensen's non-consumption: sizing a market that includes people who do nothing

### Takeaway
Christensen's canon defines non-consumption and tells you *where* to look (people blocked by skill, money, access or time, using workarounds) but states no quantitative sizing method; the only concrete sizing moves found are (a) Christensen's job-based market definition ("understanding what a company is up against in the customer's mind lets it estimate the real size of its market"), (b) the Christensen Institute's practice of counting the non-consuming population (e.g., "over 90 million people" without electricity in Nigeria), and (c) practitioners' expansions — Gurley's analog-plus-substitution sizing of Uber and Tunguz's "where will the budget come from" test.

### Cited Findings
- Definition of new-market disruption (Christensen & Raynor, quoted): "A new-market disruption is an innovation that enables a larger population of people, who previously lacked the money or skill, now to begin buying and using a product and doing the job for themselves." — quoted in [Tomasz Tunguz, "Competing Against Non-Consumption", 18 Mar 2016](https://tomtunguz.com/nonconsumption/) **[primary blog quoting the book]**
- Non-consumers accept inferior products; incumbents walk away: in stage 1 "a company opens a market segment that could not previously use any product"; "the customers in that segment can't use any of the alternative products" so they "still gladly buy your inferior product"; larger competitors "will often gladly abandon that market to you." Markets should be segmented "by the situation (i.e., the problem to be solved) and not by the attributes of the customer or product"; "Your competitors aren't just other milkshakes, but other foods commuters might consider." The notes contain no market-sizing method. — [Yevgeniy Brikman, notes on *The Innovator's Solution*, 15 May 2019](https://www.ybrikman.com/blog/2019/05/15/the-innovators-solution/) **[secondary, named]**
- Why the segment looks small: "Because this segment usually offers lower margins, it tends to look like a small market, so larger competitors will often gladly abandon it to newcomers." — [Derek Sivers, book notes on *The Innovator's Solution*](https://sive.rs/book/InnovatorsSolution) **[secondary, named]**
- Four barriers and the "fierce competitor" framing: "Nonconsumption is the inability of a person or an organization to consume a product or service"; "These barriers are time, access, skill, or money"; "New market disruptors must convert nonconsumers to consumers… They're competing against nonconsumption"; "Nonconsumption is a fierce competitor"; disruptors must "convince nonconsumers to leave their workarounds"; "building these markets first requires targeting nonconsumption." Sizing example: Nigeria has the largest population without electricity, and solar providers could "provide electricity for over 90 million people." No general quantification method is given. — [Sandy Sanchez, Christensen Institute, "Thursday Theory Tips – On Creating New Markets", 5 Oct 2023](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/blog/thursday-theory-tips-on-creating-new-markets/) **[primary (institute)]**
- Job-defined market and sizing: MIT SMR article argues most firms segment by product/customer attributes that "predict customer behavior poorly"; a market is the set of situations in which people need a job done, competitors are whatever else gets "hired", and "understanding what a company is up against in the customer's mind lets it estimate the real size of its market" (milkshake/commute example). — Christensen, Anthony, Berstell & Nitterhouse, "Finding the Right Job for Your Product", *MIT Sloan Management Review* 48(3), 1 Mar 2007, pp. 38–47, [listing](https://shop.sloanreview.mit.edu/products/48301) **[primary, paywalled; wording from the publisher's abstract and search summaries]**
- Christensen 2002 keynote framing (as reported): warned against "competing against consumption" (offering inferior products to people used to better ones) and advised to "identify potential customers who are not served by what's available today." — [Emergic, "Christensen on Innovation", 6 Oct 2002](https://emergic.org/2002/10/06/christensen-on-innovation/) **[secondary]**
- Practitioner test for non-consumption (SaaS): "Because a company's IT budget is relatively fixed" ("most CIOs foresee only a 1-2% annual increase in total IT outlay in 2016"), disruption usually means budget moving within a customer; the useful early-stage product-marketing questions are "Where will budget for this software come from?", how buyers will compare the product to alternatives, and which previously excluded users it serves. Category creators cited: Twilio, Gainsight, Duo; better-and-cheaper entrants: Slack, Asana. For mature markets he says "Westendorp Price Sensitivity and conjoint analysis" still work. No TAM figures given. — [Tomasz Tunguz, "Competing Against Non-Consumption", 18 Mar 2016](https://tomtunguz.com/nonconsumption/) **[primary]**
- Non-consumption sizing in practice (Uber): Damodaran sized Uber from "the global taxi and car-service market" (~$100B) with a 10% share cap (~$10B). Gurley: "The past can be a poor guide for the future if the future offering is materially different than the past"; price elasticity is high so "lowering the price of car-for-hire transportation will increase the usage"; San Francisco as analog — Kalanick said SF revenue was a "healthy multiple" of the historic taxi+limo market ("it's not about the market that exists, it's about the market we're creating") and heavy users' Uber spend was "somewhere north of three times as large" as their prior taxi+limo spend; car-ownership substitution — ~1B cars × ~$6,000/yr ≈ $6T, of which 2.5–12.5% capturable; expanded car-for-hire market 3–6× historic; combined TAM ≈ $450B–$1.3T. Gurley discloses he is an Uber investor/board member. — [Bill Gurley, "How to Miss By a Mile", 11 Jul 2014](https://abovethecrowd.com/2014/07/11/how-to-miss-by-a-mile-an-alternative-look-at-ubers-potential-market-size/) **[primary]**
- Market size is created, not measured: "the major reason investors passed on Shopify in the early days" was market size; Tunguz moved from Gurley's question ("whether the startup was increasing, decreasing, or maintaining the market size?") to "do we believe this company can create the market?"; "Market size is the output of all the players marketing, selling, building. It's not a given - not an output or a steady-state CAGR." — [Tomasz Tunguz, "The Market Size Mistake", 17 Apr 2024](https://tomtunguz.com/tobi-lutke-market-size/) **[primary]**
- Founder cases of under-counted non-consumption: Vanta's founders estimated ~600 companies needed SOC 2 and judged it "too small of a TAM for a compelling venture-scale business," bet demand would grow, and now have "thousands more customers than they even thought were in the company's initial market"; LaunchDarkly's VCs "doubted that the market was big enough and that developers would pay for it." Guidance: look for an audience that is "large (or will become large) but is currently underserved." — [Todd Jackson (First Round), "How to validate your startup idea", Lenny's Newsletter, 10 May 2022](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/validating-your-startup-idea) **[primary]**

### Inferences
- The Christensen-lineage sizing recipe implied by these sources: (1) define the market as job executors + job (not product buyers); (2) count everyone doing the job today *by any means* — incumbent product, workaround, or nothing — and classify non-consumers by barrier (skill/money/access/time); (3) size the money side from what the job costs today (workaround spend, ownership cost, lost outcomes) rather than incumbent category revenue; (4) use an early geography or cohort as the analog for elasticity and spend multiples (Gurley's SF; Lemkin/Blank's early customers).
- Christensen's own books give the *who* and *why*; the *how much* is supplied by practitioners (Gurley, Tunguz, Sleeper's "value theory" in KQ3/KQ4).

### Gaps
- No primary Christensen text was retrievable that states a numeric method for sizing non-consumption; the HBR 2015 "What Is Disruptive Innovation?" body was not readable ([listing](https://hbr.org/2015/12/what-is-disruptive-innovation)), and a 2023 Christensen Institute post on nonconsumption returned 404 ([christenseninstitute.org/?p=41405](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/?p=41405)).
- I found no reliable source for a quote that new-market size "cannot be measured" — treat that paraphrase as unverified.

## KQ3. Cost of the problem / value-based sizing and willingness to pay

### Takeaway
The pricing literature (Ramanujam & Tacke; Price Intelligently/Paddle; Van Westendorp; Gabor-Granger) treats WTP as something to measure *before* building, by segment, using structured questions — and treats the customer's quantified value (what the job costs today) as the ceiling that price must sit "vastly" below. The specific "hours × loaded cost × people, capture 10–25%" recipe is widespread in vendor blogs but I could not trace it to a named primary author.

### Cited Findings
- WTP talk early: "It is essential to have the 'willingness to pay' talk early in the product development process"; the book's order is "market and price, then design, then build" vs the traditional "design, build, market, and finally price." Gather (a) the overall price range "including whether customers will pay at all" and (b) WTP per feature. Conversation sequence: problem → solution → value (no price yet) → price, asking "why" after each answer. — [Yevgeniy Brikman, notes on *Monetizing Innovation* (Ramanujam & Tacke, 2016), 1 Apr 2023](https://www.ybrikman.com/blog/2023/04/01/monetizing-innovation/) **[secondary, named]**
- The WTP question set (same source): direct questions — "What is an acceptable price? An expensive price? A prohibitively expensive price? Would you buy at $XYZ?"; purchase-probability — rate 1–5, "a 5 is roughly a 50% buying probability; a 4 is roughly 10–20%", if ≤3 lower the price and repeat; most-least (show ~6 of 10 features, ask most/least valued, repeat 5–7 rounds to find "leaders" and "killers"; a killer is valued by <20% and not valued at all by >20%); build-your-own and purchase simulations "only after a rough WTP estimate exists." Segment by WTP, not demographics; traps: averages hide distribution ("an average of $60 when customers cluster at $20 and $100"), "Estimate each segment's size and cost to acquire and retain customers," and if >50% sit on the entry tier you can "land" but not "expand." Good/better/best targets: <30% good, >70% better-or-best, >10% best. — [Brikman, 2023](https://www.ybrikman.com/blog/2023/04/01/monetizing-innovation/) **[secondary, named]**
- Value capture: the book says value should be "vastly higher than the price you've set" and recommends giving customers a value-estimation tool ("product XXX saves you $5M per year!"); Brikman notes the book gives **no capture percentage**. He also cautions that asking customers to predict future behaviour "can produce misleading answers" and points to *The Mom Test* and *Continuous Discovery Habits*. — [Brikman, 2023](https://www.ybrikman.com/blog/2023/04/01/monetizing-innovation/) **[secondary, named]**
- Four monetization failures (feature shock, minivation, hidden gem, undead) — [Brikman, 2023](https://www.ybrikman.com/blog/2023/04/01/monetizing-innovation/); the "72% of innovations fail" claim appears only in podcast blurbs and is unverified — [Lenny's Podcast episode page with Madhavan Ramanujam, 8 Dec 2022](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-art-and-science-of-pricing-madhavan) (episode outline lists "What willingness to pay is and why founders should discuss it early and often," "methods for learning WTP," "how many customers to talk to," and segmentation; transcript not on page) **[primary outline only]**
- "Value theory" as a TAM method (Matrix Partners): three methods — top-down ("according to Gartner, this is a $Xbn market"; "contains remarkably little information" and "assumes that the company's disruptive product won't change the size of the TAM meaningfully"), bottom-up ("here's how we price and how many units of that price we can sell"), and value theory ("here's how much value we can add, and why we'll be able to capture it" — "more of a dark art, but nonetheless useful"). Uber value-theory sketch: compare alternatives (staying put, walking, transit, biking, taxis, driving), ask how much a user would pay to be driven instead, then "how much of that value pricing can capture"; if commoditized the company "may struggle to capture that value." He "strongly recommend[s] founders use frameworks #2 or #3." — [Jared Sleeper (Matrix Partners), "How to calculate your total addressable market and make a great TAM slide", forEntrepreneurs.com, undated (site © to 2021)](https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/calculating-tam/) **[primary]**
- WTP definition and segmentation (Paddle/ProfitWell): "Willingness to pay (WTP) is the maximum price that a customer is willing to pay for a product or service"; WTP is a range that varies by context and segment; segment by income, company size, buyer persona to build tiers. Worked data (vendor's own surveys, method undisclosed): Spotify n=7,458, stated WTP $5–$15/month vs $9.99 plan, family plan "underpriced" by ≥$3; Amazon Prime n=11,089, price path $7.99→$9.99→$12.99/month; Shopify tiers $29/$79/$299 with the top tier below WTP of merchants with $15.01M+ sales; Headspace vs Calm users with different goals had non-overlapping WTP ranges. — [Paddle, "Willingness to pay: What is WTP and how to increase it", undated](https://www.paddle.com/resources/willingness-to-pay) **[vendor]**
- Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter (as Paddle/Price Intelligently recommend it): "the best strategy to determine price sensitivity is to ask your customers a series of questions from Van Westendorp's price sensitivity meter"; the four questions — too expensive to consider; so cheap quality is doubtful; starting to get expensive but still considered; a bargain — plotted as cumulative curves whose crossings give the acceptable range and optimum ("within that center mass where customers find value from your product, don't consider it too cheap, but still consider it a good deal"); results "break… down by buyer personas." Caveat in their own guide: you "are only going to get approximations." — [Price Intelligently, "Price Sensitivity and How is it Measured"](https://www.priceintelligently.com/blog/price-sensitivity) **[vendor; page now 404 at the clean URL, content from search index]**; [Paddle, "Pricing your subscription app for growth in 2026" (Phil Carter)](https://www.paddle.com/resources/phil-carter-pricing-guide-2026-fltr) lists "Van Westendorp, Gabor-Granger, MaxDiff, and Conjoint" and notes they "leave out a very important variable: cost" **[vendor]**
- Van Westendorp question wording: "At what price would this product be so cheap that you'd question its quality?"; "At what price does this product start to seem like a good deal?"; "At what price does it begin to feel expensive?"; "At what price is it so expensive that you would not consider buying it?" Use for "Early-stage product development", benchmarking and perception; Gabor-Granger for "Optimizing pricing for launch", "Forecasting revenue", "Measuring demand elasticity." — [Syno International, "Van Westendorp vs. Gabor-Granger", 29 Sep 2025](https://synoint.com/blog/2025-09-29-van-westendorp-vs-gabor-granger-two-approaches-to-price-sensitivity-testing/) **[vendor]**
- Gabor-Granger mechanics: "Developed in the 1960s by economists André Gabor and Sir Clive Granger"; ask "Would you buy this product at X euros?", move price up on yes / down on no until the switching point; aggregate into a demand curve; revenue = price × volume and the revenue-maximizing price is "not necessarily the highest acceptable price"; sample minimum "at least 50," author recommends ≥100, 100–200 for robustness, 30–40 tolerable for niche markets; limits — respondents judge the product "in isolation" (no competition), optimizes revenue not profit; vs Van Westendorp which "defines an acceptable range without predefined prices and is used mainly for exploration." (The page's own elasticity example is arithmetically inconsistent.) — [Pierre-Nicolas Schwab, IntoTheMinds, "Gabor-Granger: Everything You Need to Know", 24 Apr 2026](https://www.intotheminds.com/blog/en/gabor-granger/) **[vendor/consultancy]**
- Fit of each survey method: Van Westendorp "often works better for novel offerings where market price expectations aren't well-established"; Gabor-Granger "is ideal when features and packaging are final and only price remains open" and "less suitable for launching a disruptive product with no equivalent"; presenting competitor prices first "produces Gabor-Granger results that more closely align with actual price-demand figures." — [Monetizely, "Van Westendorp vs Gabor-Granger for SaaS"](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/van-westendorp-vs-gabor-granger-for-saas-which-pricing-methodology-to-choose) **[vendor]**
- Cost-of-the-problem arithmetic (vendor pattern, unattributed to a named author): interview 15–20 customers across segments asking "How would you quantify the impact of solving these problems?"; value = hours saved × people × loaded hourly rate (salary + benefits), annualized; a circulating example is a 20-rep sales team saving 5 h/rep/week at $50/h loaded = $5,000/week ≈ $260,000/yr; capture bands quoted range from "10 to 25 percent" to "30–40%" of quantified annual value; validate against the next-best alternative (competitor or manual process). — [Monetizely, "Value-based pricing in SaaS: a complete guide"](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/value-based-pricing-in-saas-a-complete-guide) and [Monetizely, "How to implement value-based pricing"](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/how-to-implement-value-based-pricing) **[vendor; exact page for each figure not individually verified]**
- Bottom-up as an "ASK THEM" exercise: "Market size = # Of Customers X Revenue Per Customer"; Google how many customers exist and "ASK THEM!!!" what they would pay. — [Alex Iskold (2048 Ventures / ex-Techstars), "Everything You Need to Know About Market Sizing", startuphacks.vc, 17 Oct (year not shown)](https://www.startuphacks.vc/blog/market-size) **[primary]**
- Pricing's leverage (claim): "only 2% of SaaS companies spend significant time on pricing optimization, despite pricing having 4x the impact on profitability compared to customer acquisition" — attributed to ProfitWell by a third-party blog; original ProfitWell source not located. — [developmentcorporate.com](https://developmentcorporate.com/product-management/how-to-run-a-gabor-granger-or-van-westendorp-pricing-test-for-your-saas/) **[secondary, unverified]**

### Inferences
- The chain the method doc can draw: discovery interviews quantify the job's current cost (time, workaround spend, error/lost-revenue) → that is the value ceiling → WTP research (direct questions, PSM, Gabor-Granger) finds where customers' stated acceptable range sits beneath it → segment size × achievable price gives the value-theory TAM Sleeper describes. Ramanujam's "estimate each segment's size and cost to acquire" line is the explicit hand-off from WTP segmentation to sizing.
- Van Westendorp is the right tool when there is no reference price (new market / non-consumption); Gabor-Granger assumes a known packaging and is better for existing/resegmented markets — which lines up with Blank's market types.
- No named authority states a canonical capture ratio; Ramanujam's "vastly higher" is deliberately qualitative. Treat 10–25% as a vendor rule of thumb.

### Gaps
- Could not access *Monetizing Innovation* itself or the podcast transcript; all book detail is via Brikman's dated notes.
- Original citations for Van Westendorp (1976, ESOMAR) and Gabor & Granger (1960s) were not confirmed by any fetched page (one page gives "1960s" only).
- No named founder/PM source laying out "hours × loaded cost" with real customer data was found; only vendor templates.

## KQ4. SaaS bottom-up sizing: accounts × ACV, value metric, expansion, ICP

### Takeaway
SaaS operators converge on one build: count accounts that match the ICP (by segment/tier, from census, LinkedIn/B2B databases or install bases) × ACV per tier (or seats × price per seat, or usage × price per unit), then cross-check with the "path to $100M ARR" test (Janz's 1,000 elephants … 10M flies; Lemkin's believable 7-year model; Balfour's 100,000 × $1,000 line) rather than a top-down figure.

### Cited Findings
- Janz's $100M ladder (Point Nine): "To build a Web company with $100 million in annual revenues, you essentially need:" 1,000 elephants at $100k+ ARPA; 10,000 deer at $10k+; 100,000 rabbits at $1k+; 1,000,000 mice at $100+; 10,000,000 flies at $10+ (ads). Funnel implications: "To get to 10 million active users you need roughly 100 million people who download your app or use your website"; 1M paying consumers likely needs 10–20M trials; rabbits — with $2,700 CLTV and a 4× CLTV:CAC you can spend ~$675/customer, at 10% signup-to-paid that is ~$67.50 per signup, so 0.5–2M trial signups are needed ("the closest thing to a silver bullet is inbound marketing"); deer — 10,000 customers typically needs 100,000+ leads; elephants — PMF "several years and millions of dollars." — [Christoph Janz, "Five ways to build a $100 million business", 5 Oct 2014](https://christophjanz.blogspot.com/2014/10/five-ways-to-build-100-million-business.html) **[primary]**
- Lemkin: "To some extent in SaaS, market size doesn't matter that much … at least in the traditional top-down sense. But if you can get to $10m in any reasonable amount of time … your market *is* big enough to get you to $100m"; "Build a REAL, Bottom-Up Model, That Shows How You'll Get to $100m in ARR in 7 Years… And make sure you believe it." Model inputs: customer acquisition and mix, "realistic ACV and how it scales," pricing evidence, headcount/costs, funnel economics, funding. His own model "showed that we'd never get there ($100m) on freemium alone" (freemium ended ~20% of revenue) and implied the space could hold ~$100M ARR by 2013. Path: "$2m in ARR in 2 years… $4m the next," then $10M. — [Jason Lemkin, "TAM is Great. But What Really Matters is That You Believe You Can Hit $100m ARR in 7 Years", SaaStr, May 2013 (updated)](https://www.saastr.com/tam-is-great-but-what-really-matters-is-that-you-believe-you-can-hit-100m-in-7-years/) **[primary]**
- Lemkin on the pitch slide: "The market size number matters far less than how you calculated it"; show "Bottom-up calculation (users × price)", "Comparable products and their pricing", "Value creation versus price charged", "Logical progression from initial to expanded markets"; "'Our TAM is $50B' means nothing without showing how you arrived at that figure"; example "We're targeting 50,000 enterprise customers at an average $50K ACV, representing a $2.5B opportunity in our initial market." — [Jason Lemkin, "The Y Combinator Guide to Perfectly Pitching Your Seed Stage Startup", SaaStr, ~2025](https://www.saastr.com/the-y-combinator-guide-to-perfectly-pitching-your-seed-stage-startup-with-michael-seibel/) **[primary]**
- Lemkin on TAM exhaustion and expansion: "it's very hard to exhaust almost any TAM, even a niche TAM, until 10, 20, 30, 40 million ARR"; invest in a second product "when approaching 10% of your core Total Addressable Market"; define TAM narrowly around where most revenue comes from. — [SaaStr, "Deel is Growing 70% at $800m in ARR…"](https://www.saastr.com/deel-is-growing-70-at-800m-in-arr-does-market-share-matter-if-your-tam-is-gigantic/) **[primary]**
- Model-market fit line (Reforge): "Model Market Fit is the concept that your market (and number of customers within your market) influence your model"; "$100M requires about 100,000 customers paying $1,000 per year"; chart with ARPU on Y and customer count on X where "The diagonal line represents $100m in revenue." AI caveat: "Vendors selling seats suddenly have way fewer people to sell to, so the math of customers times ARPU breaks down"; "only 0.44% of Replit users actually want to pay"; Intercom Fin at $0.99/resolution "needs to resolve just over 100m tickets per year to find Model Market Fit." — [Brian Balfour, "The Four Fits: A Growth Framework for the AI Era", Reforge, 24 Sep 2025](https://www.reforge.com/blog/four-fits-growth-framework) **[primary]**
- Bottom-up formula and worked builds (Sleeper/Matrix): HR software at "$20 per employee per month" × target employee count; refine with employment statistics for firms with 100–5,000 staff and adjust for white-collar mix; for Uber, early SF ridership and rides-per-user "could then be extrapolated"; bottom-up "shifts the discussion to breadth of potential product market fit." — [Jared Sleeper, forEntrepreneurs](https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/calculating-tam/) **[primary]**
- Iskold's worked examples: 180,000 US dental practices × $6K ARR ($500 MRR) = ~$1B; $100M ARR needs "10% or 18,000 dental practices - and keep them!"; Netflix 120M US households × $240 = $28.8B vs ~$7B actual US revenue; 5,000 US universities × $100K = $500M ("too small" for most VCs); 100,000 US restaurants would each need to pay $10K/month to make $10B ("impossible"); "100M households paying $10/month is about $10B"; VCs like $10B+ and dislike <$1B; top-down is "typically incorrect for two reasons": too big and not your actual market (e.g., $1,600B cloud market for an ML database; $1.1T US healthcare for digital-health software), capture "1-10% MAX!" — [Alex Iskold, startuphacks.vc](https://www.startuphacks.vc/blog/market-size) **[primary]**
- Venture-scale screen: "the total addressable market (TAM) needs to be $5B or more"; "Do the math. What would have to be true for your business to reach $100M in revenue in one year?" — how many users/payers, how much each generates. Hunter Walk (Homebrew): "You can have large TAMs that actually aren't very good markets for startups, and smaller markets that can be expanded by the abilities of a talented startup with the right product offering" — evaluate the problem via LUV (Large, Urgent, Valuable). Nina Achadjian (Index): "It all comes down to the size of the market and if the business model can scale." Rachleff: "When a great team meets a lousy market, market wins." — [Lenny Rachitsky, "Your startup idea probably isn't venture-scale", 16 May 2023 (partly paywalled)](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/your-startup-idea-probably-isnt-venture) **[primary]**
- Value metric (OpenView/Poyar): a value metric is the unit that determines what customers pay (users, usage, company size); "You picked the wrong value metric" is a top-5 pricing mistake; examples — Expensify "active user" pricing, a building-software company moving from per-building to per-square-foot; "Are seats the right metric for you?" — [Blake Bartlett & Kyle Poyar, "Mastering SaaS Pricing", SaaStr Annual 2018 deck](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/mastering-saas-pricing-saastr-annual-2018/87582858) **[primary deck]**
- Seat-count TAM (Tunguz): estimates "about 57M potential seats" each for enterprise and SMB software; web3 B2B software market ≈ "$231m in 2022 & $75m excluding Ethereum" derived from the assumption "companies spend about 30% of revenue on software." — [Tomasz Tunguz, "Sizing the Web3 B2B Software Market"](https://tomtunguz.com/sizing-web3-b2b-market/) and [tomtunguz.com search results](https://tomtunguz.com/two-step-value-prop/) **[primary; seat figure from search snippet, page not fetched]**
- Investor-side formulation: "The number of customers multiplied by some sort of monetization rate (business model). This gives me a very objective way of looking at the market in terms of the customer, their willingness to pay, and the startup's ability to command a certain price"; of research reports: "What customer segments are included? What industries? Is this the total spend on SaaS?" — [The Associate (Substack), "Sizing up the market"](https://theassociate.substack.com/p/sizing-up-the-market) **[secondary]**
- Data source for counts: US Census business data "subdivided by revenue and employee count… Since most SaaS businesses focus on a set of verticals and customers of a certain size, this is an incredibly powerful resource with which to build a bottoms-up analysis"; example "roughly 40,000 mid-market SaaS companies in the US with 50 to 500 employees… At $50,000 average annual contract value, the US TAM is $2 billion." — [Launchrock, "Market Opportunity"](https://www.launchrock.com/library/expert-advice/market-opportunity) **[vendor]**
- Cross-check rule: "Ideally you will only use top-down to sanity check the magnitude of your bottom-up estimate" (Ian Taylor, Pear VC, quoted). — [Visible.vc, "Bottom-Up Market Sizing"](https://visible.vc/blog/bottom-up-market-sizing) **[vendor]**
- Tiering: "Don't use a single average. Break customers into tiers - SMB, mid-market, enterprise - and assign realistic pricing to each." — [Artisan, "How to calculate TAM"](https://www.artisan.co/blog/how-to-calculate-tam) **[vendor]**
- Account-count build with firmographic/technographic filters: "if your average ACV is $85,000 and you've identified 12,000 accounts that match your firmographic and technographic ICP, your bottom-up TAM would be approximately $1.02 billion"; narrowing an install base (every company running a given CRM) by location and size down to ~8,300 companies "worth planning against." — [HG Insights, "Total Addressable Market explained"](https://hginsights.com/blog/total-addressable-market-explained-definition-examples-and-calculations/) and [Veridion](https://veridion.com/insights/articles/how-bottom-up-company-data-sharpens-market-sizing) **[vendor]**
- Sales Navigator / Apollo / association directories / government statistics as free or cheap count sources. — [LinkedIn Sales Navigator, "TAM analysis"](https://business.linkedin.com/sell/resources/sales-terms/tam-analysis); [Clay, "Total addressable market" guide](https://marketplace.clay.com/guides/total-addressable-market) **[vendor]**

### Inferences
- The operator build is: ICP → count of accounts per tier (census/LinkedIn/install-base) → ACV per tier from early deals or WTP research (seats × price, or usage × unit price) → TAM = Σ tier count × tier ACV; SAM = tiers/geos you can serve; SOM = realistic penetration; then sanity-check against the $100M ladder (how many of which animal) and against the first 10 customers' actual ACV (Lemkin).
- Expansion (land-and-expand, usage growth) enters through ACV *trajectory* ("realistic ACV and how it scales" — Lemkin; "over 50% on entry tier → can land but not expand" — Ramanujam), not through a separate TAM line.

### Gaps
- OpenView's own TAM articles ("Modeling Your Total Addressable Market [Free Template]", "5 Quick Ways to Evaluate Your Market Size", "The Science of B2B Market Sizing") now redirect to the firm's homepage; the formula "targetable opportunities × average selling price" and the definitions (total market potential / TAM / SAM / expected share) are known only from search snippets of the latter guide ([original URL](https://openviewpartners.com/blog/b2b-market-sizing)).
- David Skok has no sizing post of his own on forEntrepreneurs that I found; the TAM article there is by Jared Sleeper (Matrix).
- No Tunguz post gives a generic accounts × ACV walkthrough; his sizing posts are case-specific.
- Point Nine: beyond Janz's ladder, no Point Nine market-sizing guide was found.

## KQ5. PM-course / product-institute framings and the "customers × value × reachability" formula

### Takeaway
PM authorities treat market size as one question among several (Cagan's #3 of 10; Torres's "sizing" lens of four), to be answered conservatively with bottom-up data, and none publishes a sizing method. The "customers × value × reachability" style formula shows up as practitioner/course heuristics (Users affected × improvement rate × value; addressable % × reachable % × capture %), not as a formula attributable to Perri, Cagan, Torres or Reforge.

### Cited Findings
- Cagan's opportunity assessment: ten questions — "Exactly what problem will this solve? (value proposition)", "For whom do we solve that problem? (target market)", "How big is the opportunity? (market size)", alternatives, differentiator, "Why now?", go-to-market, metrics/revenue, critical success factors, go/no-go. On market size he suggests input from "industry analysts, trade associations, your finance staff" and "your own bottom up calculations"; "be conservative and realize that not every opportunity needs to be a billion dollar market"; sizing is "a topic in itself" and not covered further. — [Marty Cagan, "Assessing Product Opportunities", SVPG, 13 Dec 2006](https://www.svpg.com/assessing-product-opportunities/) **[primary]**
- Torres's assessment lenses: "Assessing opportunities is the process of evaluating and prioritizing which customer needs, pain points, and desires to address based on their potential to drive your desired outcome." Sizing questions: "How many customers are impacted?" and "How often does this need arise?"; market factors ("How would addressing this opportunity affect your position in the market?"), company factors (fit with vision/strategy), customer factors ("How important is this to customers? And how satisfied are they with existing solutions?"), plus impact on outcome. "These are subjective, messy decisions"; "One opportunity might win on one dimension and lose on another"; they are "two-way door decisions" testable by prototyping. — [Product Talk, "Assessing Opportunities" (glossary), updated 25 Oct 2025](https://www.producttalk.org/glossary-discovery-assessing-opportunities/) **[primary]**
- Torres on evidence base: opportunities on the tree should be limited to those heard in interviews ("we guarantee that we are working on real unmet customers needs"); you need "at least three customer interviews" to build a tree and should start mapping after "three to four customer stories"; interview widely to see whether distinct segments exist — "if their needs are exactly the same, you can remove the segments from your tree." — [Product Talk, "Prioritize Opportunities, Not Solutions"](https://www.producttalk.org/prioritize-opportunities/); [Product Talk, "Representing Customer Segments on Your Opportunity Solution Tree"](https://www.producttalk.org/customer-segments-teresas-take/); [Product Talk, "Opportunity Mapping"](https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-mapping/) **[primary]**
- Reforge: no dedicated market-sizing essay found; the Four Fits piece supplies the customers × ARPU line (see KQ4), and Reforge's SEO guide frames opportunity sizing as "similar to a TAM (total addressable market) sizing, but based instead on the SEO traffic up for grabs." — [Reforge, "SEO Opportunity Sizing"](https://www.reforge.com/guides/size-seo-opportunity-sizing) **[primary]**
- Shopify data-science framing: template "if we build feature X, we will acquire MM (+/- delta) new active users in T timeframe under DD assumptions"; use ranges, document each assumption, use a comparable system, size new initiatives conservatively, and use "an annualized view of the impact so you can easily compare across initiatives." Worked example: 100,000 new Italian leads/year; a localized French email had 5% CTR vs 3% non-localized, and a 5% CTR increase produced a 3% rise in active users → ~3,000 more active users/year. — [Ella Hilal (VP Data Science, Shopify), "Shopify Data Guide: Opportunity Sizing", 25 Aug 2022](https://shopify.engineering/shopify-data-guide-opportunity-sizing) **[primary]**
- Course heuristics for reach × value: "Use Users Affected x Improvement Rate x Value to size any product bet with real numbers, not gut feel" — [Maven lesson, "Opportunity sizing in Claude Code"](https://maven.com/p/6fe402/opportunity-sizing-in-claude-code) **[course marketing]**; reach estimate from adoption benchmarks ("if a product has 100,000 users and similar features typically see 30% adoption, teams can expect around 30,000 users") and separating opportunity sizing (reach) from impact estimation (depth) — [Uxcel, "Data-driven roadmapping"](https://uxcel.com/lessons/data-driven-roadmapping-391) **[course]**; stacked percentages "addressable % (who needs your product), reachable % (what your channels can reach), and capture rate % (what you convert)" with "early-stage SaaS typically converts 2-5% of reachable leads" — [startupa.ge TAM/SAM/SOM calculator](https://startupa.ge/tools/market-sizing-calculator) **[vendor tool]**
- Hunter Walk's problem-first screen: LUV = Large, Urgent, Valuable; "identifying a large, urgent, and valuable (LUV) problem" rather than chasing a large market size. — quoted in [Lenny Rachitsky, 16 May 2023](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/your-startup-idea-probably-isnt-venture) **[primary]** and summarized in [Product Mindset, "LUV framework"](https://productmindset.substack.com/p/luv-large-urgent-and-valuable-framework) **[secondary]**
- Ulwick/Strategyn market definition as sizing unit: the job executor "is the customer using the product to get a functional job done" (homeowner, job "protecting the home from the environment"), buyers/installers/support don't define the market; "Core markets defined in this manner are stable over time, as they are solution independent"; adjacent markets = new job executors for current jobs, or new jobs for current executors; "there are tens of thousands of markets out there." No counting method given. — [Tony Ulwick, "Bring Markets Into Perfect Focus by Defining Them Around the Customer's Job-To-Be-Done", Strategyn, 12 Jul 2016](https://strategyn.com/bring-markets-perfect-focus-defining-around-customers-job-done/) **[primary]**

### Inferences
- The PM-side formula the method doc can state honestly is a composite: Opportunity ≈ (job executors in segment) × (frequency/intensity of the job) × (value per execution or per year) × (share reachable through available channels), with Torres supplying the first two terms from interview evidence, Ramanujam/Sleeper the value term, and funnel heuristics the reachability term. No single named author owns this.
- Cagan and Torres both make sizing a *gate* question answered conservatively, consistent with Lemkin/Blank's view that early evidence (first customers, interviews) outweighs top-down figures.

### Gaps
- Nothing from Melissa Perri / Product Institute on opportunity or market sizing was found in search; her public material (Escaping the Build Trap, Product Kata) was not accessible to confirm whether it addresses sizing at all.
- No Reforge essay on TAM/market sizing exists in the public index I could search.
- I could not identify who first stated a "customers × value × reachability" formula in those words.

## KQ6. Worked examples with real numbers from PM / founder sources

### Takeaway
The most reusable worked builds are Janz's $100M ladder (customers × ARPA by tier, with funnel multiples), Iskold's four count × price examples (dental, Netflix, universities, restaurants), Gurley's Uber non-consumption build (analog multiple + substitution of ownership cost), Lemkin's three-horizon user × ARPU × penetration build for vibe coding, and Shopify's funnel-based feature sizing.

### Cited Findings
- B2B vertical SaaS (dental): 180,000 US practices × $6K ARR = ~$1B; $100M ARR = 18,000 practices (10%) retained. University SaaS: 5,000 × $100K = $500M (judged too small). Consumer: 120M US households × $240 = $28.8B (Netflix US revenue ~$7B, ~¼). Restaurants: 100,000 × $10K/month needed for $10B — "impossible." Rule of thumb: ~100M households × $10/month ≈ $10B. — [Alex Iskold, startuphacks.vc](https://www.startuphacks.vc/blog/market-size) **[primary]**
- $100M ladder with funnel multiples (1,000 × $100k … 10M × $10; rabbits need 0.5–2M trial signups at $67.50/signup allowable; deer need 100,000+ leads). — [Christoph Janz, 5 Oct 2014](https://christophjanz.blogspot.com/2014/10/five-ways-to-build-100-million-business.html) **[primary]**
- New-market sizing from analog + substitution (Uber): historic taxi/limo ~$100B → expanded car-for-hire 3–6× → plus 1B cars × $6,000/yr × 2.5–12.5% → TAM ≈ $450B–$1.3T; SF spend "north of three times" prior taxi+limo spend among heavy users. — [Bill Gurley, 11 Jul 2014](https://abovethecrowd.com/2014/07/11/how-to-miss-by-a-mile-an-alternative-look-at-ubers-potential-market-size/) **[primary]**
- Three-horizon user × ARPU × penetration (vibe coding): H1 ~20M users (5M founders, 3M freelancers, 2M SMB owners, 8M students/hobbyists, 2M developers) × $300–600/yr × "30-40% over 3 years"; H2 ~65M × $2,000–8,000 × 25–35% over 5–7 yrs; H3 ~1.1B × $200–2,000 × "30-60% over 10+ years"; scenarios $50B / $200B / $400B+ by 2030. Note: the post's stated dollar ranges ($2–12B H1, $40–300B H2) do not reconcile with its own inputs (recomputed ≈ $1.8–4.8B and $32.5–182B). — [Jason Lemkin, "The Vibe Coding TAM", SaaStr, ~Jul 2025](https://www.saastr.com/the-vibe-coding-tam-how-big-can-this-market-really-get/) **[primary; arithmetic inconsistent]**
- Enterprise pitch example: "50,000 enterprise customers at an average $50K ACV, representing a $2.5B opportunity." — [Jason Lemkin, SaaStr YC pitching guide](https://www.saastr.com/the-y-combinator-guide-to-perfectly-pitching-your-seed-stage-startup-with-michael-seibel/) **[primary]**
- Model-market fit: $100M = 100,000 customers × $1,000/yr; Intercom Fin at $0.99/resolution needs "just over 100m tickets per year." — [Brian Balfour, Reforge, 24 Sep 2025](https://www.reforge.com/blog/four-fits-growth-framework) **[primary]**
- Feature-level funnel sizing: 100,000 Italian leads/yr; CTR 3→5% comparable; 3% lift in active users → ~3,000 users/yr. — [Ella Hilal, Shopify, 25 Aug 2022](https://shopify.engineering/shopify-data-guide-opportunity-sizing) **[primary]**
- Early-customer demand signals used instead of TAM: Good Dog estimated "several hundred thousand breeders in the U.S." and had 50K+ signed up/onboarding by end-2021; Vanta's ~600-company initial estimate; Flexport's 300+ mock-landing-page signups; Pinwheel's 133 inbound leads in 72 hours; Cocoon started with parental leave for 100–200-employee companies in three states though "all types of employers… needed a leave solution." — [Todd Jackson, Lenny's Newsletter, 10 May 2022](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/validating-your-startup-idea) **[primary]**
- Per-seat HR example: "$20 per employee per month" × employees at firms with 100–5,000 staff, adjusted for white-collar mix. — [Jared Sleeper, forEntrepreneurs](https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/calculating-tam/) **[primary]**
- Vendor worked builds (useful as templates, not evidence): EU fintech SaaS — 12,000 ICP accounts × 40 seats × €360 = €172.8M revenue TAM, 25% adoption over 5 yrs → €43.2M SOM ([Topo.io](https://www.topo.io/blog/what-is-tam-total-addressable-market)); US HR-tech — ~180,000 companies with 50–500 employees actively hiring × $12,000/yr, 4% capture in 5 yrs ≈ $86M ARR ([Causo](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/tam-slide-market-size)); $85,000 ACV × 12,000 ICP accounts ≈ $1.02B ([HG Insights](https://hginsights.com/blog/total-addressable-market-explained-definition-examples-and-calculations/)); 40,000 US mid-market SaaS companies × $50,000 = $2B ([Launchrock](https://www.launchrock.com/library/expert-advice/market-opportunity)); 100,000 potential customers × $2,000/yr = $200M ([Visible.vc](https://visible.vc/blog/bottom-up-market-sizing/)). **[vendor]**
- University-programme example of a tiny market: a student team "estimated their product's market at $25,000 a year." — [Pitt Innovation Institute, 27 Jun 2016](https://blog.innovation.pitt.edu/how-big-is-the-pie) **[secondary]**

### Inferences
- Every credible worked build exposes three auditable inputs — a count with a stated source, a price with a stated basis (early deals, comparables, WTP research), and a penetration/time assumption — and the named-author examples (Iskold, Janz, Lemkin, Balfour) use the $100M-ARR reachability test as the pass/fail, which is a reachability check expressed in revenue rather than a separate "SOM %."
- Gurley's and Jackson's cases show that when the market is new, early-cohort data (SF revenue multiple; Vanta's customer count outrunning its 600 estimate) replaces the count input, which is the practical answer to "how to size non-consumption."

### Gaps
- No founder write-up walking step-by-step through a LinkedIn Sales Navigator count with real filter outputs was found; vendor guides describe the method only.
- Lemkin's vibe-coding figures are internally inconsistent and should be cited only as an illustration of the structure (users × ARPU × penetration by horizon), not for the numbers.
