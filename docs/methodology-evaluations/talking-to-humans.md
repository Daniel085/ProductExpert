# Methodology Evaluation — Talking to Humans

**Methodology:** *Talking to Humans* (Giff Constable with Frank Rimalovski)
— qualitative customer discovery in the Steve Blank / Customer Development
lineage, with the authors' companion materials (*10 Tips to Remember*,
*Assumptions Exercise*, *Teaching Exercises*).
**Scope:** the book's method as distilled in
`methods/customer-interviews/talking-to-humans.md` (source PDFs and
extracted text under `materials/`). The Fluxx research-and-insight
principles that the `customer-interviews` agent also reads are *not*
scored here — this is the book.
**Rubric:** [`rubric.md`](./rubric.md). **Recorded:** 2026-09-18.

---

## Summary

**Totals: 4 Fully supported / 16 Some support / 5 Not well.**

| Cluster | Profile | Read |
|---------|---------|------|
| **1.x Problem / framing** | 3 Fully / 7 Some / 0 Not | broad — the only method with no problem-batch hole |
| **2.x Solution** | 0 Fully / 5 Some / 2 Not | validates one hypothesis; generates none |
| **3.x Market / commercial** | 1 Fully / 4 Some / 3 Not | stops at the persevere/pivot call |

**The shape is a *validation* method: few peaks, few holes.** It is
*Fully supported* where discovery discipline lives — identifying who to
learn from (1.1), naming the problem as a testable assumption (1.2),
**fighting bias** (1.4 — the strongest anti-bias *craft* of the four), and
**cheap validation** (3.4 — the cheapest of the four). It never scores Not
well in the problem batch because the Assumptions Exercise touches
customer, problem, value, channel, revenue, competition, edge, and
viability, and synthesis ends in an explicit decision. Its holes are
consistent: it **generates nothing** (2.1), **estimates nothing** (2.6),
and **stops at the decision** — no positioning (3.5), no KPIs (3.7), no
positioning-based gate (3.8).

**Verdict for a product discovery process.** The best evidence discipline
and bias-proofing of the four, and the cheapest to run. Use it as the
**validation spine** of discovery. Pair it with a framing/ideation method
(Design Thinking, JTBD, or the `ideation` agent) in front, ODI behind it
for quantification, and a business-case layer (`prfaq`, `metrics`) after.

**In ProductExpert terms:** this is the canon of the `customer-interviews`
agent, deliberately extended with the Fluxx insight bar, job stories, and
the shared assumption ledger — the extensions patch its 1.3 (reframing)
and 1.5 (stakeholder expression) partials.

## Batch 1 — The problem

| # | Criterion | Rating |
|---|-----------|--------|
| 1.1 | Identifies people affected | **Fully supported** |
| 1.2 | Identifies the problem | **Fully supported** |
| 1.3 | Challenges the problem (XY) | **Some support** |
| 1.4 | Challenges our understanding (bias) | **Fully supported** |
| 1.5 | Expresses problem for stakeholders | **Some support** |
| 1.6 | Quantifies problem importance | **Some support** |
| 1.7 | Quantifies confidence in analysis | **Some support** |
| 1.8 | Applies across scenarios (regulatory/partner/VOIP) | **Some support** |
| 1.9 | Handles non-user-based situations | **Some support** |
| 1.10 | Clear go/no-go criteria (problem) | **Some support** |

**1.1 — Fully supported.** Assumptions Exercise prompts #1 (target
customer) and #7 (early adopter); tip #2 "be smart about who you target —
work from your hypotheses on market and early adopters, not a shotgun
approach"; a screener; buyers distinguished from users and both sides of a
two-sided market. *Caveat:* a customer-chain lens — stakeholders outside
the buyer/user/influencer chain are not enumerated.

**1.2 — Fully supported.** "The problem" is an explicit assumption (#2) to
be validated; the question bank anchors it in "tell me about the last
time…"; prompt #4 (why not solved today) probes why it persists; synthesis
maps observations back to the problem assumption and updates conviction.

**1.3 — Some support.** It rigorously challenges *whether* the problem is
real — hunt to disprove, why-not-today, how they solve it now — and
stories often surface a different problem than the one hypothesized. But
there is no reframing tool: the method validates the hypothesis you
brought, and reframing is a by-product of good listening rather than a
step.

**1.4 — Fully supported.** The book is a bias-fighting manual: detective
not salesperson (~95% listening), never pitch, behavior over opinions and
stories over speculation, "compliments are not data — commitment is,"
"prepare yourself to hear things you don't want to hear," observation kept
separate from interpretation, named biases (confirmation, sample,
loudest-voice, recency), saturation as the stopping rule. ODI's bias
control is statistical; this is the strongest *craft*-level control of the
four.

**1.5 — Some support.** The synthesis read-out — patterns with head-counts,
assumption scorecard, decision — is legible and decision-oriented. But the
method is not built for stakeholder narrative: no personas, journeys, or
story artifacts; the scorecard persuades analysts more than executives.

**1.6 — Some support.** No measurement, but two explicit ordinal
mechanisms: assumptions prioritized by **impact × uncertainty**, and
evidence weighted by **commitment** (time, money, data, a referral) over
stated enthusiasm — a qualitative importance ladder that maps loosely onto
WIBNI → committed. Importance is ranked, not quantified.

**1.7 — Some support.** A **conviction score** (1–5) per assumption,
updated on evidence; head-counts per pattern; saturation. Explicit and
tracked, but a belief score, not a measured confidence.

**1.8 — Some support.** The interview craft transfers to any human
respondent — buyers, channel partners, technical decision-makers — so
partner and regulatory *stakeholders* are interviewable, and its explicit
buyer/user/influencer treatment makes it better than Design Thinking for
B2B. The technical core of a VOIP problem isn't interviewable; the
human-locus limit remains.

**1.9 — Some support.** Its unit is "a human you can learn from" —
customer, buyer, or expert (the cold-call-the-expert drill) — which is
broader than an end-user, so non-user-but-human situations (a partner's
procurement, a compliance officer's job) are in-method. Situations with no
human locus are not.

**1.10 — Some support.** An explicit decision step — **persevere / pivot /
dig deeper** — driven by the scorecard and commitment signals. A named
decision with qualitative inputs; no thresholds.

## Batch 2 — The solution

| # | Criterion | Rating |
|---|-----------|--------|
| 2.1 | Explore multiple theoretical options | **Not well** |
| 2.2 | Plausible solution set within business priorities | **Some support** |
| 2.3 | Clear set of outcomes the solution delivers | **Some support** |
| 2.4 | Clear value proposition | **Some support** |
| 2.5 | Applies across scenarios (UX → detailed VOIP flows) | **Some support** |
| 2.6 | Identifies effort involved | **Not well** |
| 2.7 | Go/no-go criteria (solution) | **Some support** |

**2.1 — Not well.** Not an ideation method: it tests the solution
hypothesis you have and warns you against pitching it. No option
generation.

**2.2 — Some support.** The Assumptions Exercise frames the one solution
inside business assumptions — revenue, acquisition channel, competition,
edge, viability and technical risk — so plausibility-within-business is
checked. A *set* is never produced.

**2.3 — Some support.** Prompt #5 (measurable outcome) plus interview
probes on what customers actually get; qualitative and single-hypothesis.

**2.4 — Some support.** Value is probed from the customer's side — why
they'd care, what they'd commit, whether they'd pay (the revenue
assumption) — with competition and edge as commercial inputs. A validated
value *hypothesis*, not a positioning-grade value proposition.

**2.5 — Some support.** It designs nothing at any level; validation
conversations work wherever a human can react (a technical buyer to a
call-flow concept), so applicability is by respondent, not by artifact.

**2.6 — Not well.** "Technical risk" and "viability" are named as
assumptions to check, not estimated; no sizing method.

**2.7 — Some support.** Persevere/pivot applies to the solution hypothesis
too, and **commitment currency** — pre-orders, data access, referrals — is
a real go/no-go signal for it. Qualitative; no threshold.

## Batch 3 — The market

| # | Criterion | Rating |
|---|-----------|--------|
| 3.1 | Resolves conflicting requirements | **Some support** |
| 3.2 | Picks the best solution for the market | **Some support** |
| 3.3 | Sizes the opportunity (revenue + constraints) | **Some support** |
| 3.4 | Cheap ways to validate assumptions pre-build | **Fully supported** |
| 3.5 | Identifies market positioning | **Not well** |
| 3.6 | Applies across scenarios (SaaS → on-prem) | **Some support** |
| 3.7 | Identifies success KPIs | **Not well** |
| 3.8 | Go/no-go given market positioning | **Not well** |

**3.1 — Some support.** Early-adopter primacy is the tie-breaker — serve
the early adopter first, mainstream later — a prioritization principle,
not a conflict-resolution method; nothing for stakeholder, regulatory, or
technical conflicts.

**3.2 — Some support.** Market pull is tested directly through commitment
and early-adopter identification — a genuine market signal for one
candidate. No comparison across candidates, no competitive or economic
analysis.

**3.3 — Some support.** Revenue, pricing, and acquisition are explicit
assumptions (#6, #8) and willingness to pay is probed — sizing *inputs*;
no sizing method or constraint analysis.

**3.4 — Fully supported.** The thesis of the book: learn before you build.
Assumptions map to the cheapest evidence; mock interviews and the
cold-approach drill make even the practice cheap; a commitment ask is the
cheapest possible validation of demand; the Lean Startup lineage supplies
validated learning. The cheapest validation of the four.

**3.5 — Not well.** Competition (#9) and edge (#10) are validated as
assumptions — richer inputs than Design Thinking offers — but there is no
positioning method: no category, alternatives frame, or messaging.

**3.6 — Some support.** Deployment-agnostic; interviews are exactly the
tool to learn the buyer constraints (procurement, IT policy, data
residency) that decide SaaS vs. on-prem, but the method has nothing to say
about the trade-off itself.

**3.7 — Not well.** The "measurable outcome" prompt seeds one customer-side
metric; no KPI set or measurement framework.

**3.8 — Not well.** No positioning, so no positioning-based gate.
Commitment-based gating (1.10, 2.7) exists but is not positioning-aware.
