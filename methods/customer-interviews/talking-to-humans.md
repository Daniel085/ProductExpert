## What customer discovery is for

Customer discovery replaces **assumptions** with **evidence from real people**,
*before* you spend money building. You are testing beliefs, not collecting a
feature wishlist. The output is sharper conviction — or a pivot — **not** a
requirements document.

The goal of an interview is **the truth, not a yes.**

---

## Mindset (the part most people get wrong)

- **Get out of the building.** Real learning lives outside your own head and
  your team's opinions. (Steve Blank's core mantra.)
- **Be a detective, not a salesperson.** You're there to learn, not to validate
  yourself or sell. If you're talking more than ~20% of the time, you're doing
  it wrong.
- **Hunt for truth, not validation.** Actively try to *disprove* your idea. Be
  willing — eager, even — to hear bad news early, when it's still cheap.
- **Behavior over opinions.** What someone *has actually done* predicts the
  future far better than what they *say they would do*.
- **Stories over speculation.** Anchor every topic in a specific, recent, real
  event — not a hypothetical.
- **Compliments are not data.** "Cool idea, I'd totally use that" is noise.
  Commitment (time, money, reputation, a referral) is signal.
- **You're looking for problems, patterns, and intensity** — not applause.

---

## The discovery loop

1. **Assumptions** — Write down what must be true for the idea to work.
2. **Prioritize** — Rank by **risk × uncertainty**; attack the riskiest,
   least-known "leap-of-faith" assumptions first.
3. **Who** — Define the customer segment(s) and how you'll recognize and recruit
   them.
4. **Ask** — Run interviews that surface stories about real behavior.
5. **Synthesize** — Find patterns; update conviction per assumption.
6. **Decide** — Persevere, pivot, or dig deeper. Then repeat.

## Phase 1 — Prep

### 1. Frame the learning goal
One sentence: *what do you need to learn, and what decision will it inform?*
Vague goals produce vague interviews. "Find out if people want this" is not a
goal; "learn how mid-market ops managers currently reconcile invoices, and
whether it's painful enough to switch tools" is.

### 2. Surface and rank your assumptions
Brainstorm everything that must be true. Useful buckets:
- **Customer** — who they are; that this segment exists and is reachable.
- **Problem** — that the problem is real, frequent, and painful.
- **Value** — that they'd change behavior / pay / switch to solve it.
- **Channel** — that you can reach and acquire them.
- **Context** — workflow, budget, who else is involved in the decision.

Then plot each on **risk × uncertainty**:
- *Risk:* if this is wrong, how badly does the idea break?
- *Uncertainty:* how little do we actually know?

Attack the **high-risk, high-uncertainty** assumptions first. Those are your
leap-of-faith assumptions, and interviews exist to test them.

### 3. Define who you're talking to
Write 1–3 **customer archetypes** — specific enough to recruit against and
recognize on a call. In a two-sided market, you have archetypes on *both* sides.
Turn each archetype into a short **screener** (3–5 questions) so you talk to the
right people, not just the easy-to-reach ones.

### 4. Write the interview guide
A guide, **not a script.** Plan a flow of topics and your best opening
questions, then follow the conversation. Aim for ~8–12 core questions over
~30–45 minutes. Structure:
- **Warm-up / framing** — set them at ease; "no wrong answers; I'm not selling
  anything; I want to learn how you actually work."
- **Context** — their role, their world, how the relevant activity fits in.
- **The story** — walk through the last real time the problem occurred.
- **Dig** — why it was hard, what it cost, what they tried, workarounds.
- **Wrap** — anything you should have asked; a referral to others like them.

Hold your solution back. If you must show it, do it **last**, and frame it to
invite criticism.

### 5. Recruiting & outreach
- **Where:** your network and theirs (snowball / referrals), LinkedIn, niche
  communities, events, relevant forums, intercepts.
- **The ask:** keep it small and specific ("20 minutes, I'm researching how
  teams do X, not selling anything"). Respect their time. Make it easy to say
  yes.
- Beware **sample bias** — friends and fans tell you what you want to hear.

### 6. Logistics
- **Pair up** when you can: one interviewer, one note-taker.
- **Record** (with permission) so you can stay present.
- **Book a debrief slot** right after each interview — memory decays fast.

## Interviewing craft — do / don't

**Do**
- Ask **open-ended** questions.
- "**Tell me about the last time** you [did the activity]."
- Ask **why**, repeatedly — get beneath the first answer.
- **Embrace silence.** Count to three; let them fill it.
- Ask how they solve it **today**: tools, hacks, workarounds, and what it costs.
- Let them go on tangents — that's where the gold is.
- Listen for **emotion and intensity** (a painkiller, not a vitamin).

**Don't**
- **Pitch.** The moment you sell, they stop telling you the truth.
- **Lead:** "Wouldn't it be great if…?" / "Don't you hate it when…?"
- **Ask hypotheticals:** "Would you use…?" / "Would you pay…?" People are
  terrible at predicting their own future behavior — ask about the past instead.
- **Ask them to design your product.** Understand the problem; design is your job.
- **Accept compliments as evidence.**
- **Ask yes/no questions** when an open one would reveal more.

## Question bank (starters — adapt, don't recite)

- "Walk me through the **last time** you [did the activity]. What happened, step
  by step?"
- "What's the **hardest part** about [X]?"
- "**Why** is that hard? What does it cost you — time, money, stress?"
- "What have you **tried** to solve it? What tools or workarounds do you use?"
- "What happens **if you don't** solve it?"
- "Who **else** is involved when this comes up?"
- "Last time you looked for a better way, what did you do?"
- "Is there anything I **should have asked** you about this?"
- "Who else do you know who deals with this that I should talk to?"

> Replace any hypothetical ("Would you…?") with its behavioral twin ("When did
> you last…?").

## Phase 2 — Synthesis

### Debrief immediately
Within a few minutes of hanging up, capture: top **surprises**, the best verbatim
**quotes**, what **confirmed or contradicted** each assumption, and any **new
questions** to chase.

### Separate observation from interpretation
Keep two columns:
- **Observation** — what they said or did (fact, ideally a quote).
- **Interpretation** — what you infer it means (a hypothesis, not a fact).

Most analysis mistakes are interpretations smuggled in as observations.

### Find patterns across interviews
Cluster observations into themes. For each theme, note **how many people**
support it. Distinguish **signal from noise** — one loud voice is not a pattern.
Watch for **saturation**: when new interviews stop surprising you, you're near
done for that segment.

### Map back to your assumptions
For each assumption, mark: **validated / invalidated / still unknown / new**, and
update a **conviction** score (e.g., 1–5). Weigh **commitment** signals
(did anyone give time, money, data, or a referral?) above stated enthusiasm.

### Decide
Make the call the discovery was *for*: **persevere, pivot, or dig deeper** — and
name the **next test**.

### Watch your biases
- **Confirmation bias** — hearing only what supports the idea.
- **Sample bias** — only friends, fans, or the easy-to-reach.
- **Loudest-voice bias** — over-weighting one vivid story.
- **Recency bias** — over-weighting the last conversation.

---

## How many interviews?

This is qualitative, not statistical. Keep going until **patterns repeat and
surprises stop** (saturation) — often roughly a dozen per segment, but it
varies. Optimize for **decisions**, not sample size.
