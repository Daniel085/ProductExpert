## What customer discovery is for

Customer discovery replaces **assumptions** with **evidence from real people**,
*before* you spend money building. You are testing beliefs, not collecting a
feature wishlist. The output is sharper conviction — or a pivot — **not** a
requirements document. The goal of an interview is **the truth, not a yes.**

---

## Mindset (the part most people get wrong)

- **Get out of the building.** Real learning lives outside your own head and
  your team's opinions. (Steve Blank's core mantra.)
- **Be a detective, not a salesperson.** You're there to learn, not to sell.
- **Listen — 95% of the conversation should be them talking.** Don't be afraid
  of silences; let them think. *(Talking to Humans, "10 Tips")*
- **Prepare yourself to hear things you don't want to hear**, and don't let your
  excitement and optimism bias what you hear.
- **Behavior over opinions; stories over speculation.** Get stories about real,
  past behavior — don't ask people to speculate ("would you pay for X?").
- **Ask for advice.** It disarms people and gets them helping you think.
- **Don't pitch** — unless you're actually trying to close for real money.
- **Compliments are not data.** Commitment (time, money, a referral) is signal.
- **Look for patterns and use judgement; don't take any one conversation
  literally.**

---

## The discovery loop

1. **Assumptions** — Write down what must be true for the idea to work.
2. **Prioritize** — Mark the assumptions that are **high-impact AND highly
   uncertain**; those are your customer-discovery priorities.
3. **Who** — Define the segment(s) and early adopters, and how to reach them.
4. **Ask** — Run interviews that surface stories about real behavior.
5. **Synthesize** — Find patterns; update conviction per assumption.
6. **Decide** — Persevere, pivot, or dig deeper. Then repeat.

## Phase 1 — Prep

### 1. Frame the learning goal
One sentence: *what do you need to learn, and what decision will it inform?*
"Find out if people want this" is not a goal; "learn how mid-market ops managers
reconcile invoices today, and whether it's painful enough to switch" is.

### 2. Surface and prioritize your assumptions — the official exercise
Work through the *Talking to Humans* **Assumptions Exercise**. Make each answer
"as concise and specific as possible" so you can run an experiment against it:

1. My **target customer** will be?
2. The **problem** my customer wants to solve is?
3. My customer's need can be **solved with**? (your concise elevator pitch)
4. **Why can't** my customer solve this today? (the obstacles)
5. The **measurable outcome** my customer wants to achieve is?
6. My primary **customer-acquisition** tactic will be?
7. My **earliest adopter** will be?
8. I will make **money (revenue)** by?
9. My primary **competition** will be?
10. I will **beat competitors** primarily because of?
11. My biggest **financial-viability risk** is?
12. My biggest **technical/engineering risk** is?

Then answer the killer question: **"What assumptions do we have that, if proven
wrong, would cause this business to fail?"** (list them). **Final step:** mark
the assumptions that have a **large impact** on the business *and* feel **highly
uncertain** — those are your priorities for customer discovery and the
experiments to run.

### 3. Define who you're talking to
Write 1–3 **customer archetypes**, specific enough to recruit against and
recognize on a call. Work from your hypotheses on market and **early adopters** —
you can't reach the mainstream without early adopters first. In a two-sided
market, you have archetypes on *both* sides. Turn each into a short **screener**
(3–5 questions) so you talk to the right people, not just the easy-to-reach ones.
Be smart about who you target; **don't take a shotgun approach, talking to
anyone with a pulse.**

### 4. Write the interview guide
A guide, **not a script** — don't feel you have to rigidly stick to it. Go in
prepared: know your goals and questions ahead of time, but **don't carry an
endless list of questions.** Aim for a handful of strong, open-ended questions
(~5–10) over ~30–45 minutes, structured:
- **Warm-up / framing** — set them at ease; "no wrong answers; I'm not selling
  anything; I want to learn how you actually work."
- **Context** — their role, their world, how the relevant activity fits in.
- **The story** — walk through the last real time the problem occurred.
- **Dig** — why it was hard, what it cost, what they tried, workarounds.
- **Wrap** — what you should have asked; **ask for introductions** to more people.

Hold your solution back. If you must show it, do it **last**, framed to invite
criticism.

### 5. Recruiting & outreach
- **Where:** your network and theirs (snowball / referrals), LinkedIn, niche
  communities, events, forums, intercepts.
- **The ask:** small and specific ("20 minutes, I'm researching how teams do X,
  not selling anything"). Respect their time. Make it easy to say yes.
- **Cold approaches** (the "cold-call the expert" drill): keep the intro **short
  and clear**, **praise and disarm** the person, **don't pitch too soon**, and
  **don't imply they have a problem and desperately need your help.** Those are
  the classic mistakes that get you hung up on.
- Beware **sample bias** — friends and fans tell you what you want to hear.

### 6. Logistics
- **Talk to one person at a time.** If you bring a note-taker, they stay quiet.
- **No focus groups** — they manufacture consensus and drown out individual truth.
- **Record** (with permission) so you can stay present.
- **Book a debrief slot** right after each interview — memory decays fast.

## Question bank (starters — adapt, don't recite)

Two useful angles (from the authors' teaching exercises): questions that **dig up
past behavior**, and questions that **unlock deeper motivations**.

**Past behavior**
- "Walk me through the **last time** you [did the activity], step by step."
- "Tell me about the **last thing you bought** to deal with this."
- "What's the **hardest part** about [X]? **Why** is that hard? What did it cost
  you — time, money, stress?"
- "What have you **tried** to solve it? What tools or workarounds do you use?"
- "What happens **if you don't** solve it?" / "Who **else** is involved?"

**Deeper motivation**
- "When did you first start caring about [X], and why?"
- "Of the reasons you shared, **why** is that one the most important to you?"

**Always close with**
- "Is there anything I **should have asked**?"
- "Who else like you should I talk to?"

> Replace any hypothetical ("Would you…?") with its behavioral twin
> ("When did you last…?").

## Practice (rehearse before you go live)

From the authors' **Teaching Exercises** — use these to sharpen the PM, not the
customer:
- **Mock interview.** Pick a relatable topic, plan ≤6 questions, then role-play:
  one interviewer (who also takes notes), one subject, ~5–7 minutes, then debrief
  what was leading, hypothetical, or over-pitched.
- **Mock approach ("cold-call the expert").** Practice the opening over and over;
  a partner "blocks" misguided attempts (long/unclear intro, pitching too soon,
  implying they need help) until the approach **praises and disarms** the expert
  and earns the conversation.

## Phase 2 — Synthesis

### Debrief immediately
Within minutes of hanging up: top **surprises**, best verbatim **quotes**, what
**confirmed or contradicted** each assumption, and **new questions** to chase.

### Separate observation from interpretation
- **Observation** — what they said or did (fact, ideally a quote).
- **Interpretation** — what you infer it means (a hypothesis, not a fact).

Most analysis mistakes are interpretations smuggled in as observations.

### Find patterns across interviews
Cluster observations into themes; note **how many people** support each.
**Look for patterns and use judgement — don't take any one conversation
literally.** Distinguish signal from noise; watch for **saturation** (new
interviews stop surprising you).

### Map back to your assumptions
For each: **validated / invalidated / still unknown / new**, and update a
**conviction** score (e.g., 1–5). Weight **commitment** (time, money, data, a
referral) above stated enthusiasm.

### Decide
Make the call the discovery was *for*: **persevere, pivot, or dig deeper** — and
name the **next test**.

### Watch your biases
Confirmation bias · sample bias (only friends/fans) · loudest-voice bias ·
recency bias.

---

## How many interviews?

Qualitative, not statistical. Keep going until **patterns repeat and surprises
stop** (saturation) — often roughly a dozen per segment, but it varies. Optimize
for **decisions**, not sample size.
