A '''minimum viable product''' ('''MVP''') is a version of a product with just enough features to be usable by early customers who can then provide feedback for future [[new product development|product development]].<ref>{{Cite web | url=https://www.techopedia.com/definition/27809/minimum-viable-product-mvp |title = What is a Minimum Viable Product (MVP)? – Definition from Techopedia}}</ref><ref name="Ries, Eric">{{cite web
| url           = http://www.startuplessonslearned.com/2009/08/minimum-viable-product-guide.html
| title         = Minimum Viable Product: a guide
| author        = Ries, Eric
| date          = August 3, 2009
}}</ref>

A focus on releasing an MVP means that developers potentially avoid lengthy and (possibly) unnecessary work. Instead, they iterate on working versions and respond to feedback, challenging and validating assumptions about a product's requirements.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.slalom.com/insights/mvp-maximally-misunderstood-term|url-status=dead|title=MVP: A maximally misunderstood idea|website=Slalom|language=en|access-date=2019-12-04|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190328192411/https://www.slalom.com/insights/mvp-maximally-misunderstood-term|archive-date=2019-03-28|author-first1=Arbi|author-last1=Vartan|author-first2=Jeff|author-last2=Brinkerhoff}}</ref> The term was coined and defined in 2001 by Frank Robinson<ref>{{cite web|title=SyncDev methodology|url=http://www.syncdev.com/minimum-viable-product/|website=SyncDev|accessdate=May 16, 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160525101214/http://www.syncdev.com:80/minimum-viable-product/ |archive-date=May 25, 2016 |url-status=dead}}</ref> and then popularized by [[Steve Blank]] and [[Eric Ries]].<ref name="def">W. S. Junk, "[http://www.cs.pomona.edu/~markk/cs181f.f12/supp/DynamicBalance.pdf The Dynamic Balance Between Cost, Schedule, Features, and Quality in Software Development Projects]", Computer Science Dept., University of Idaho, SEPM-001, April 2000.</ref><ref>Eric Ries, March 23, 2009, [http://startuplessonslearned.blogspot.com/2009/03/minimum-viable-product.html Venture Hacks interview: "What is the minimum viable product?"], Lessons Learned</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://steveblank.com/2010/03/04/perfection-by-subtraction-the-minimum-feature-set/|title=Steve Blank Perfection By Subtraction – The Minimum Feature Set|date=March 4, 2010|website=Steve Blank}}</ref><ref>[[Ryan Holiday|Holiday, Ryan]] [https://thenextweb.com/entrepreneur/2015/04/01/the-single-worst-marketing-decision-you-can-make/ The single worst marketing decision you can make] ''The Next Web''. 1 April 2015</ref> It may also involve carrying out [[market analysis]] beforehand. The MVP is analogous to experimentation in the scientific method applied in the context of validating business hypotheses. It is utilized so that prospective entrepreneurs would know whether a given business idea would actually be viable and profitable by testing the assumptions behind a product or business idea.<ref name=":0">{{Cite web|title=A Minimum Viable Product Is Not a Product, It's a Process: Building Product, Experimentation, MVP|url=https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4Q-a-minimum-viable-product-is-not-a-product-it-s-a-process|access-date=2020-10-17|website=YC Startup Library|language=en}}</ref> The concept can be used to validate a market need for a product<ref name=":0" /> and for incremental developments of an existing product.<ref>{{Cite web|title=The Scientific Method for Startups: Building Product, Experimentation, KPI|url=https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4g-the-scientific-method-for-startups|access-date=2020-10-17|website=YC Startup Library|language=en}}</ref> As it tests a potential business model to customers to see how the market would react, it is especially useful for new/startup companies who are more concerned with finding out where potential business opportunities exist rather than executing a prefabricated, isolated business model.

== Description ==
[[File:From minimum viable product to more complex product.png|thumb|346x346px|A diagram that illustrates how one can start develop and launch a minimum viable product by implementing at first the most simple, usable product with the most essential functionality.]]
A minimum viable product has just enough core features to effectively deploy the product, and no more. Developers typically deploy the product to a subset of possible customers, such as [[early adopter]]s who are thought to be more forgiving, more likely to give feedback, and able to grasp a product vision from an early prototype or marketing information. This strategy aims to avoid building products that customers do not want and seeks to maximize information about the customer with the least money spent. The technique falls under the ''lean startup'' methodology as MVPs aim to test business hypotheses and validated learning is one of the five principles of [[the Lean Startup]] method.<ref>{{Cite web|last=|first=|date=|title=Eric Ries on 'The Lean Startup'|url=https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/eric-ries-on-the-lean-startup/|archive-url=|archive-date=|access-date=|website=Knowledge @ Wharton}}</ref> It contrasts strongly with the traditional "[[stealth mode]]" method of product development where businesses make detailed business plans spanning a considerable time horizon. Steve Blank posited that the main principle of the Lean Startup approach rests in the validation of the hypotheses underlying the product by asking customers if they want the product or if the product meets their needs, and pivoting to another approach if the hypothesis turns out to be false.<ref name=":1">{{Cite news |last=Blank |first=Steve |date=2013-05-01 |title=Why the Lean Start-Up Changes Everything |work=Harvard Business Review |issue=May 2013 |url=https://hbr.org/2013/05/why-the-lean-start-up-changes-everything |access-date=2020-10-17 |issn=0017-8012}}</ref> This approach to validating business ideas cheaply before substantial investment saves costs and limits risk as businesses that upon experimentation turn out to be commercially unfeasible can easily be terminated. It is especially important as the main cause of startup failure is the lack of market need;<ref>{{Cite web|date=2019-11-06|title=Why Startups Fail: Top 20 Reasons l CB Insights|url=https://www.cbinsights.com/research/startup-failure-reasons-top/|access-date=2020-10-19|website=CB Insights Research|language=en-US}}</ref> that is, many startups fail because their product isn't needed by many people, and so they cannot generate enough revenue to recoup the initial investment. Thus it can be said that utilizing an MVP would illuminate a prospective entrepreneur on the market demand for their products.

For example, in 2015, specialists from the [[University of Sydney]] devised the Rippa robot to automate farm and weed management.<ref>{{Cite web|title=Rippa robot takes farms forward to the future|url=https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2015/10/21/rippa-robot-takes-farms-forward-to-the-future-.html|access-date=2020-10-19|website=The University of Sydney|language=en-AU}}</ref> Before it was released, the technical hypothesis – that the robot can distinguish weeds from farm plants – had already been proven. But the business hypothesis – that it would be a viable tool on a working farm – still needed to be proved.<ref>{{Cite web|last=admin|date=2018-07-26|title=RIPPA The Farm Robot Exterminates Pests And Weeds|url=https://agronomybots.com/index.php/2018/07/26/rippa-the-farm-robot-exterminates-pests-and-weeds/|access-date=2020-10-19|website=agronomybots|language=en-US|archive-date=2020-10-19|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201019232625/https://agronomybots.com/index.php/2018/07/26/rippa-the-farm-robot-exterminates-pests-and-weeds/|url-status=dead}}</ref> The application of the MVP method here is that the business hypothesis is tested, and only if it proves successful will further development be invested.

"The minimum viable product is that version of a new product a team uses to collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort."<ref name="Ries, Eric"/> The definition's use of the words maximum and minimum means it is not formulaic. It requires judgment to figure out, for any given context, what MVP makes sense. Due to this vagueness, the term MVP is commonly used, either deliberately or unwittingly, to refer to a much broader notion ranging from a rather prototype-like product to a fully-fledged and marketable product.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.projectmanagement.com/blog-post/61937/Defining-MVP--MBI--MMF--and-MMR|title=Defining MVP, MMF, MMP, and MMR|last1=Ambler|first1=Scott|website=The Disciplined Agile (DA) Blog|accessdate=19 June 2021|date=2017-12-27}}</ref>

An MVP can be part of a strategy and process directed toward making and selling a product to customers.<ref name="MVP">{{cite web|url=http://radoff.com/blog/2010/05/04/minimum-viable-product-rant/|title=Minimum Viable Product rant|last1=Radoff|first1=Jon|date=May 4, 2010|website=Jon Radoff's Internet Wonderland|archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20140323181121/http://radoff.com/blog/2010/05/04/minimum-viable-product-rant/|archivedate=March 23, 2014|url-status=unfit|accessdate=19 August 2014}}</ref> It is a core artifact in an iterative process of idea generation, prototyping, presentation, data collection, analysis and learning. One seeks to minimize the total time spent on an iteration. The process is iterated until a desirable [[product/market fit]] is obtained, or until the product is deemed non-viable.

===Testing===
Testing is the essence of minimum viable products. As described above, an MVP seeks to test out whether an idea works in market environments while using the least possible expenditure. This would be beneficial as it reduces the risk of innovating (so that enormous amounts of capital would not have to be sacrificed before proving that the concept does not actually work), and allowing for gradual, market-tested expansion models such as the real options model.<ref>{{Cite news|last1=Putten|first1=Alexander B. van|last2=MacMillan|first2=Ian|date=2004-12-01|title=Making Real Options Really Work|work=Harvard Business Review|issue=December 2004|url=https://hbr.org/2004/12/making-real-options-really-work|access-date=2020-10-31|issn=0017-8012}}</ref> A simple method of testing the financial viability of an idea would be discovery-driven planning,<ref>{{Cite news|last1=McGrath|first1=Rita Gunther|last2=MacMillan|first2=Ian|date=1995-07-01|title=Discovery-Driven Planning|work=Harvard Business Review|issue=July–August 1995|url=https://hbr.org/1995/07/discovery-driven-planning|access-date=2020-10-31|issn=0017-8012}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news|last=Gallo|first=Amy|date=2017-02-13|title=A Refresher on Discovery-Driven Planning|work=Harvard Business Review|url=https://hbr.org/2017/02/a-refresher-on-discovery-driven-planning|access-date=2020-10-31|issn=0017-8012}}</ref> which first tests the financial viability of new ventures by carefully examining the assumptions behind the idea by a reverse [[income statement]] (first, begin with the income you want to obtain, then the costs the new invention would take, and see if the required amount of revenue that must be gained for the project to work). Results from a minimum viable product test aim to indicate if the product should be built, to begin with. Testing evaluates if the initial problem or goal is solved in a manner that makes it reasonable to move forward.

Testing an MVP typically involves releasing the product to a limited group of users to gather feedback on functionality, usability, and value.<ref>{{cite web |title=Minimum Viable Product (MVP) |url=https://www.atlassian.com/agile/product-management/minimum-viable-product |website=Atlassian |access-date=2026-04-01}}</ref> The results are used to guide further development and iteration.

=== Marketing ===
The MVP differs from the conventional market testing strategy of investing time and money early to implement a product before testing it in the market. It is intended to ensure that the market ''wants the product'' before large time and monetary investments are made. The MVP differs from the [[open-source software]] methodology of ''[[release early, release often]]'' that listens to users, letting them define the features and future of the product. Instead, the MVP starts with a product vision, which is maintained throughout the product life cycle, although it is adapted based on the explicit and implicit (indirect measures) feedback from potential future customers of the product.<ref name="Ries, Eric" />

== Criticism ==
Some research has shown that early release of an MVP may hurt a company more than help when companies risk imitation by a competitor and have not established other barriers to imitation.<ref name="Contigiani">{{cite web |title=The Limitations of Lean Startup Principles |url=http://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/the-limitations-of-lean-startup-principles/ |website=knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu |publisher=Knowledge@Wharton, [[Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania]] |date=28 September 2018 |accessdate=17 October 2018}}</ref> It has also indicated that negative feedback on an MVP can negatively affect a company's reputation.<ref name="Contigiani"/> Many developers of mobile and digital products are now criticizing the MVP because customers can easily switch between competing products through platforms (e.g. app stores).<ref>{{cite web |last=Beneyto |first=Carlos |url=https://medium.com/swlh/the-mvp-is-dead-long-life-to-the-map-minimum-awesome-product-404df90fef7f |title=The MVP is dead, long life to the MAP. (Minimum Awesome Product)|date= February 5, 2018 }}</ref> Also, products that do not offer the expected minimum standard of quality are inferior to competitors that enter the market with a higher standard.

A notable limitation of the MVP is rooted in its approach that seeks out to test its ideas to the market. Since the business's new product ideas can be inferred from their testing, the method may be unsuited to environments where the protection of the intellectual property is limited (and where products are easily imitated).<ref>{{Cite web|last=Contigiani|first=Andrea|date=|title=The Downside of Applying Lean Startup Principles|url=https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/the-limitations-of-lean-startup-principles/|archive-url=|archive-date=|access-date=|website=Knowledge @ Wharton}}</ref>

The criticism of the MVP approach has led to several new approaches, e.g. the minimum viable experiment MVE,<ref>{{cite web |url = https://blog.validately.com/death-minimal-viable-product/ |title = Death to the Minimum Viable Product! {{!}} Lean User Testing |date = January 11, 2019 |access-date = January 22, 2019 |archive-date = September 29, 2018 |archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20180929073738/https://blog.validately.com/death-minimal-viable-product/ |url-status = dead }}</ref> the minimum awesome product MAP,<ref>{{cite web |url=https://fluidmobile.de/leistungen/app-strategie-digitalisierungsstrategie/minimum-awesome-product/ |title=Minimum Awesome Product in der App-Entwicklung |author=fluidmobile GmbH |date=January 22, 2019 |access-date=January 22, 2019 |archive-date=January 9, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190109062728/https://fluidmobile.de/leistungen/app-strategie-digitalisierungsstrategie/minimum-awesome-product/ |url-status=dead }}</ref> or the simple, lovable, complete.<ref>{{cite web | url=https://longform.asmartbear.com/slc/ | title=I hate MVPs. So do your customers. Make it SLC instead. | last=Cohen | first=Jason | date=22 Aug 2017}}</ref>
